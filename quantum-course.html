Foundations

Everything in the course is built from four objects: a state, an operation, a measurement, and the probabilities that connect them.

Key points
  • A qubit state is a unit vector \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\). The squared sizes \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\) are the measurement probabilities.
  • Gates are unitary matrices. Measurement is random and cannot be undone.
  • A global phase has no effect. A relative phase is what algorithms use.

The single qubit

A qubit lives in a two-dimensional complex vector space. Its state is a unit-length column vector, written in Dirac notation as

\[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|0\rangle + \beta\,|1\rangle,\qquad |0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad |1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Here \(\psi\) (psi) names the state, and \(\alpha,\beta\) (alpha, beta) are complex numbers called amplitudes. Amplitudes are not probabilities; their squared magnitudes are. A measurement in the computational basis returns 0 with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) and 1 with probability \(|\beta|^2\). So every physical state obeys the normalization condition \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\).

Three things you can do to a qubit

  • Evolve it with a unitary matrix \(U\): the new state is \(|\psi'\rangle = U|\psi\rangle\).
  • Measure it. The superposition collapses to one basis outcome, drawn with the Born-rule probabilities.
  • Ask for an expectation value: the average result of an observable. A simulator can compute it exactly; hardware can only estimate it by sampling.

Phase is the whole game

Two states that differ only by an overall factor \(e^{i\gamma}\) (a global phase; \(\gamma\) is gamma) are physically identical. No measurement can tell them apart.

A relative phase between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) is different. A computational-basis measurement cannot see it, but it changes the outcome in other bases. Interference uses it, and most of this course is about placing, moving, and reading phases.

Reading the notation

Circuits are drawn left to right. Each wire is a qubit, gates are boxes, and [M] marks a measurement. In code, PennyLane is imported as qp (the usual alias is qml). Code blocks show the essential lines, not the boilerplate.

A convention that catches everyone: PennyLane numbers qubits left to right, so \(|10100\rangle\) puts wires 0 and 2 in \(|1\rangle\). Many textbooks and other frameworks make qubit 0 the rightmost bit. Always check the ordering when you use a new library.

The physics underneath

The code treats a qubit as a vector and a gate as a matrix, but the vector describes something real. This section is the quantum mechanics that the exercises assume.

Key points
  • Energy comes in discrete packets (quanta). Amplitudes are complex numbers, so they can cancel or add: this is interference.
  • Four postulates define the theory: state, unitary evolution, measurement, and the tensor product.
  • Observables are Hermitian matrices. Observables that do not commute cannot both have definite values.
  • Decoherence and the no-cloning theorem are why hardware is hard.

Where quantum mechanics comes from

Around 1900, classical physics failed to explain some experiments. Hot objects did not glow the way theory predicted, and light knocked electrons out of metals in discrete lumps. Planck and Einstein proposed that energy comes in indivisible packets, called quanta, with \(E=h\nu\).

Here \(\nu\) (nu) is the frequency and \(h\) is Planck's constant, \(6.626\times10^{-34}\) J·s. This tiny number sets the scale at which the world stops being smooth.

Why the amplitudes are complex

A quantum system is described by a state vector (in continuous systems, a wavefunction) in a Hilbert space: a complex vector space with an inner product. A complex number has both a size and an angle (a phase). When two paths to the same outcome have amplitudes that point in opposite directions, they cancel; when they align, they add. That is interference, as in the double-slit experiment, and real probabilities alone cannot do it.

What superposition means

A qubit in \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) is in a superposition. The accurate statement is not "it is both 0 and 1 at once." It is that the system has a definite amplitude for each outcome, and only a measurement forces a single result. A quantum algorithm arranges for the wrong answers' amplitudes to cancel and the right answer's amplitude to survive.

The four postulates

  • Postulate 1 · StateA system is a unit vector in a Hilbert space. For one qubit, \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) with \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\). See I.1.
  • Postulate 2 · EvolutionA closed system evolves by a unitary. \(|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle\). In continuous time this is the Schrödinger equation \(i\hbar\,\tfrac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = \hat H|\psi\rangle\), where \(\hat H\) (the Hamiltonian) is the energy operator. See I.3–I.8.
  • Postulate 3 · MeasurementMeasurement is random and irreversible. The result is an eigenvalue of the observable. Its probability is the squared amplitude along the matching eigenvector (the Born rule), and the state collapses to that eigenvector. See I.9–I.10.
  • Postulate 4 · Composite systemsSystems combine by tensor product. Two qubits live in the 4-dimensional space \(\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2\). Entanglement becomes possible here. See I.11.

Observables and eigenvalues

A measurable quantity is represented by a Hermitian operator: a matrix equal to its own conjugate transpose, \(A=A^\dagger\). Its eigenvalues are always real, so they can be meter readings. Its eigenvectors are orthogonal, so the outcomes can be told apart.

The Pauli operators have eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\), so every Pauli expectation value lies in \([-1,1]\). The expectation value \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle\) is the average reading over many identical runs.

Incompatible observables

Two observables can be measured together only if they commute (\(AB=BA\)). The Paulis do not commute with each other. So a state with a definite \(Z\) value, such as \(|0\rangle\), has a completely uncertain \(X\) value. This is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, and it is why you must choose a basis to measure in (see I.9).

The Bloch sphere

Ignoring global phase, any single-qubit state can be written with two angles:

\[ |\psi\rangle=\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle. \]

\(\theta\) (theta) is a polar angle and \(\varphi\) (phi) an azimuthal angle, so every state is a point on a sphere: the Bloch sphere. The north pole is \(|0\rangle\), the south pole is \(|1\rangle\), and the equator holds \(|\pm\rangle\) and \(|y_\pm\rangle\). The gates \(RX,RY,RZ\) are rotations about the x-, y-, and z-axes.

The half angle \(\theta/2\) appears because amplitudes turn at half the rate of the Bloch vector. A full \(2\pi\) rotation returns the Bloch vector to its start but multiplies the state by \(-1\).

Entanglement

Postulate 4 allows two-qubit states that are not a product of single-qubit states. These are entangled, such as the Bell state \((|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Measuring one qubit fixes the other. Shared classical randomness can fake that correlation in one basis. It cannot fake the full pattern of correlations across incompatible measurement settings: that is Bell's theorem.

Decoherence and no-cloning

A real qubit is coupled to its environment, which keeps "measuring" it and scrambles its relative phases. This is decoherence. It is why devices are noisy and why I.10 works with finite shots. The no-cloning theorem says you cannot copy an unknown quantum state, so you cannot make backups either.

I.1

All about qubits

Build the qubit by hand in NumPy: a normalized vector, an inner product, a measurement drawn from probabilities, and a unitary applied as a matrix.

I.1.1

Normalization of quantum states

An arbitrary combination \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) is not yet a physical state. Normalize it by dividing by its norm \(\sqrt{|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2}\), so the squared amplitudes sum to one.

Solution 
state = alpha * ket_0 + beta * ket_1
norm  = np.sqrt(np.abs(alpha)**2 + np.abs(beta)**2)
return state / norm
I.1.2

Inner product & orthonormal bases

The inner product \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) measures the overlap of two states. The bra \(\langle\phi|\) is the conjugate transpose of the ket. Checking \(\langle0|0\rangle=\langle1|1\rangle=1\) and \(\langle0|1\rangle=0\) shows that \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\) is orthonormal.

Solution 
# the bra ⟨s1| is the conjugate transpose of the ket |s1⟩
return np.dot(np.conj(state_1), state_2)
I.1.3

Sampling measurement outcomes

A measurement is a random draw. The probabilities are the squared amplitudes, so you simulate many measurements by sampling 0 or 1 from that distribution.

Solution 
probs = np.abs(state)**2
return np.random.choice([0, 1], size=num_meas, p=probs)
I.1.4

Applying a quantum operation

Operations are unitary matrices, and the new state is the matrix–vector product \(U|\psi\rangle\). Here \(U\) is the Hadamard matrix, which is its own inverse: \(U(U|0\rangle)=|0\rangle\).

Solution 
U = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
return np.dot(U, state)
I.1.5

A simple quantum algorithm

Every quantum program has three steps: initialize, apply, measure. Chaining the three functions above gives the full pipeline in one line.

Solution 
return measure_state(apply_u(initialize_state()), 100)
I.2

Quantum circuits

PennyLane does the matrix bookkeeping. You write a circuit as a Python function, bind it to a device as a QNode, and call it. Gate order matters, and so does depth.

I.2.1

Order of operations

Gates in a quantum function run top to bottom, so the line order must match the circuit's left-to-right order. A circuit is a sequence, not a set.

circuit
|0> --*--[H]--(+)--------[M]
      |        |
|0> --(+)------|--[RY]---[M]
               |
|0> --[RX]-----*---------[M]
Solution 
qp.CNOT(wires=[0, 1])
qp.RX(theta, wires=2)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.CNOT(wires=[2, 0])
qp.RY(phi, wires=1)
return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])
I.2.2

Building a QNode

A QNode binds a quantum function to a device, so you can call it like a normal function. qp.probs returns the probability of every output basis state.

Solution 
dev = qp.device("default.qubit", wires=3)

def my_circuit(theta, phi, omega):
    qp.RX(theta, wires=0); qp.RY(phi, wires=1); qp.RZ(omega, wires=2)
    qp.CNOT(wires=[0,1]); qp.CNOT(wires=[1,2]); qp.CNOT(wires=[2,0])
    return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])

my_qnode = qp.QNode(my_circuit, dev)
I.2.3

The QNode decorator

The usual shortcut is the decorator @qp.qnode(dev). It makes a QNode of the same name, already bound to the device.

Solution 
@qp.qnode(dev)
def my_circuit(theta, phi, omega):
    ...
    return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])
I.2.4

Circuit depth

Depth is the number of layers that must run in sequence: the longest path through the circuit's dependency graph. Gates on different wires share a layer; a gate waits only for its own wires.

Layer 1: RX(θ), RY(φ), RZ(ω)   (parallel, different wires)
Layer 2: CNOT(0,1)
Layer 3: CNOT(1,2)             (waits for wire 1)
Layer 4: CNOT(2,0)             (waits for wire 2)   →  depth = 4
Check, don't guess: qp.specs(my_circuit)(...)['resources'].depth returns the depth PennyLane computes.
I.3

Unitary matrices

Any unitary can go straight into a circuit, either as a raw matrix or, for one qubit, as three angles.

I.3.1

Unitaries in PennyLane

qp.QubitUnitary applies any matrix to the wires you choose. You supply the unitary; the device applies it (compare the hand calculation in I.1.4).

Solution 
U = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
qp.QubitUnitary(U, wires=0)
I.3.2

Parametrized unitaries: the Rot gate

Every single-qubit unitary can be written with three real parameters. That general form is the Rot gate:

\[ U(\phi,\theta,\omega)=\begin{pmatrix} e^{-i(\phi+\omega)/2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} & -e^{i(\phi-\omega)/2}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\\[4pt] e^{-i(\phi-\omega)/2}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} & e^{i(\phi+\omega)/2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix} \]
Solution 
qp.Rot(phi, theta, omega, wires=0)
I.4

X and H

Pauli X flips a bit; Hadamard makes a superposition. Combining them already shows a gate identity.

I.4.1

Flipping bits

Qubits start in \(|0\rangle\). The Pauli X gate swaps \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), so it is the standard way to prepare \(|1\rangle\).

Solution 
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.QubitUnitary(U, wires=0)
I.4.2

Uniform superposition

The Hadamard sends \(|0\rangle\) to \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). That is why it opens nearly every quantum algorithm.

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.4.3

Combining X and H

\(H|0\rangle=|+\rangle\) and \(H|1\rangle=|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Both give probabilities \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\); they differ only by a relative phase.

Solution 
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.4.4

A QNode with X and H: a hidden Z

The identity HXH = Z: X between two Hadamards is a Pauli Z. Gate identities like this make circuit compilation possible.

circuit
|0> --[H]--[X]--[H]--[M]        (= Pauli Z)
Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.5

It's just a phase

The Z family leaves computational-basis probabilities alone and changes only relative phase. Meet Z, its continuous version RZ, and the fixed fractions S and T.

I.5.1

The Pauli Z gate

Z sends \(|0\rangle\to|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\to-|1\rangle\). On \(|+\rangle\) it gives \(|-\rangle\). The probabilities stay \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\).

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)   # make |+⟩
qp.PauliZ(wires=0)     # → |−⟩
I.5.2

The Z rotation

RZ(ω) sends \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) to \(e^{-i\omega/2}\alpha|0\rangle+e^{i\omega/2}\beta|1\rangle\): a relative phase \(e^{i\omega}\) on \(|1\rangle\). With \(\omega=\pi\) it equals Pauli Z up to the global phase \(-i\).

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.RZ(np.pi, wires=0)  # = Z up to global phase −i
I.5.3

The S and T gates & adjoints

The quarter turn \(RZ(\pi/2)\) is the S gate; the eighth turn \(RZ(\pi/4)\) is the T gate (both up to global phase). qp.adjoint gives the inverse, and \(RZ^\dagger(\omega)=RZ(-\omega)\).

circuit
|0> --[H]--[S]--[T†]--[RZ(0.3)]--[S†]--[M]
Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.S(wires=0)
qp.adjoint(qp.T)(wires=0)
qp.RZ(0.3, wires=0)
qp.adjoint(qp.S)(wires=0)
I.6

From a different angle

RX and RY rotate about the other two axes. Watch the amplitudes oscillate and the half angle appear.

I.6.1

Applying RX

RX(π) equals Pauli X up to the global phase \(-i\): \(RX(\pi)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-i\\-i&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=-iX\).

Solution 
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.RX(np.pi, wires=0)
I.6.2

Plotting RX

\(RX(\theta)|0\rangle=\cos\frac\theta2|0\rangle-i\sin\frac\theta2|1\rangle\). The \(|0\rangle\) amplitude is real, the \(|1\rangle\) amplitude is imaginary, and both repeat with period \(4\pi\) because of the half angle.

Solution 
qp.RX(theta, wires=0)
return qp.state()
I.6.3

Plotting RY

RY keeps everything real: \(RY(\theta)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2 & -\sin\frac\theta2\\ \sin\frac\theta2 & \cos\frac\theta2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Use it when you want real coefficients.

Solution 
qp.RY(theta, wires=0)
return qp.state()
I.7

Universal gate sets

A few gates are enough to build everything. Two rotations, or the discrete pair {H, T}, reach any single-qubit unitary.

I.7.1

Universality of rotations

Rot(φ,θ,ω) is RZ·RY·RZ, and any single-qubit unitary also decomposes as RZ·RX·RZ. So {RZ, RY} or {RZ, RX} is a universal gate set for one qubit. The Hadamard is three quarter turns:

\[ H=e^{i\pi/2}\,RZ(\tfrac\pi2)\,RX(\tfrac\pi2)\,RZ(\tfrac\pi2) \]
Solution 
phi, theta, omega = np.pi/2, np.pi/2, np.pi/2
qp.RZ(phi, wires=0); qp.RX(theta, wires=0); qp.RZ(omega, wires=0)
I.7.2

Synthesizing a circuit

Rewrite the circuit \(H,\ S,\ T^\dagger,\ Y\) over {RZ, RX}. As an operator it is the product \(Y\,T^\dagger S\,H\). The ZXZ Euler decomposition says three rotations are enough. Matching against \(RZ(\omega)RX(\theta)RZ(\phi)\) gives \(\phi=-\tfrac\pi2,\ \theta=\tfrac\pi2,\ \omega=-\tfrac{3\pi}4\).

Solution 
qp.RZ(-np.pi/2, wires=0)
qp.RX(np.pi/2, wires=0)
qp.RZ(-3*np.pi/4, wires=0)
I.7.3

Universality of H and T

{H, T} approximates any single-qubit unitary to any precision. Here the target is exact in six gates: the \((1/\sqrt2)^3\) prefactor shows three Hadamards. This is circuit synthesis, part of quantum compilation.

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0); qp.T(wires=0); qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.T(wires=0); qp.T(wires=0); qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.8

Prepare yourself

The inverse problem: given a target state, find a short circuit that builds it. Do it by hand for one qubit, and use a template when it gets hard.

I.8.1

State preparation

Target \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}|0\rangle+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}e^{i5\pi/4}|1\rangle\). A Hadamard gives the equal superposition, then RZ(5π/4) places the relative phase (up to a global phase).

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.RZ(5*np.pi/4, wires=0)
I.8.2

State preparation, revisited

Target \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2|0\rangle-\tfrac{i}2|1\rangle\). One RX does it: \(\cos\frac\theta2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\) gives \(\theta=\pi/3\). The RX signature (real \(|0\rangle\), imaginary \(|1\rangle\)) saves gates.

Solution 
qp.RX(np.pi/3, wires=0)
I.8.3

State prep with Möttönen's method

Templates are subroutines you use like a gate. qp.StatePrep prepares any normalized vector. Drawing at level="device" shows the Möttönen RY/RZ sequence it expands into.

Solution 
qp.StatePrep(state, wires=0)
# inspect the decomposition:
qp.draw(prepare_state, level="device")(v)
I.9

Measurements

Return probabilities directly, and learn the one trick for measuring in any basis: rotate that basis onto the computational one first.

I.9.1

Measuring a superposition

qp.probs returns outcome probabilities. H on either \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) gives \([0.5, 0.5]\): the minus sign in \(|-\rangle\) is invisible in this basis.

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
return qp.probs(wires=0)
I.9.2

Y-basis rotation

The eigenvectors of Pauli Y are \(|y_\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). S·H builds them: H makes \(|\pm\rangle\), then S puts the factor \(i\) on \(|1\rangle\).

Solution 
def y_basis_rotation():
    qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
    qp.S(wires=0)
I.9.3

Measurement in the Y basis

The general recipe: to measure in another basis, apply the adjoint of the rotation that creates that basis, then measure normally.

Solution 
prepare_psi()
qp.adjoint(y_basis_rotation)()
return qp.probs(wires=0)
I.10

What did you expect?

Expectation values are how quantum programs report answers. A simulator computes them exactly; hardware estimates them from shots, and the error shrinks in a predictable way.

I.10.1

Measurement of PauliY

Return qp.expval of an observable. A Pauli expectation value always lies in \([-1,1]\), between the eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\).

circuit
|0> --[RX(π/4)]--[H]--[Z]--<Y>
Solution 
return qp.expval(qp.PauliY(0))   # PauliY(0) is shorthand for wires=0
I.10.2

Setting up the number of shots

Hardware samples. The shot count is set on the device, and as it grows the estimate converges to the exact value \(-1/\sqrt2\approx-0.7071\).

Solution 
dev = qp.device("default.qubit", wires=1, shots=shots)
# shots = 100, 1000, ... 1e6  →  estimate → −0.7071
I.10.3

Evaluating the samples

qp.sample returns raw outcomes. The only results are \(\pm1\), so the expectation value is their mean: \(\langle Y\rangle=\big(\#(+1)-\#(-1)\big)/N\).

Solution 
n_plus  = np.count_nonzero(samples == 1)
n_minus = np.count_nonzero(samples == -1)
return (n_plus - n_minus) / len(samples)
I.10.4

The variance of sample measurements

For H then \(\langle Z\rangle\), one shot is \(\pm1\) with equal probability: mean 0, variance 1. Averaging \(N\) shots divides the variance by \(N\), so the error bar shrinks as \(1/\sqrt N\):

\[ \mathrm{Var}[\langle Z\rangle]=\frac{1}{N_{\text{shots}}} \]
Solution 
results = np.array([circuit() for _ in range(n_trials)])
return np.var(results)          # experiment
# theory:  estimated_variance = 1 / n_shots
I.11

Multi-qubit systems

Two qubits, four dimensions. Prepare basis states, measure several observables, combine them with the tensor product, and see why a product state's joint expectation factorizes.

I.11.1

Preparing basis states

PennyLane indexes wires left to right. qp.BasisState takes a bit list, so writing an integer in binary gives \(|n\rangle\): \(3\to[0,1,1]\to|011\rangle\).

Solution 
bits = [int(b) for b in np.binary_repr(basis_id, width=num_wires)]
qp.BasisState(bits, wires=range(num_wires))
I.11.2

Separable operations

Build \(|+\rangle\otimes|1\rangle\) and return two expectation values as a tuple. \(|+\rangle\) is on the Bloch x-axis, so \(\langle Y\rangle=0\); \(|1\rangle\) gives \(\langle Z\rangle=-1\).

Solution 
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)          # |+⟩
qp.PauliX(wires=1)            # |1⟩
return qp.expval(qp.PauliY(0)), qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(1))
I.11.3

A two-qubit observable

Combine observables with @ for the tensor product. On \(|1\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\), \(\langle Z\rangle=-1\) and \(\langle X\rangle=-1\), so \(\langle Z\otimes X\rangle=1\).

Solution 
qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.PauliX(wires=1); qp.Hadamard(wires=1)   # |1⟩|−⟩
return qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(0) @ qp.PauliX(1))
I.11.4

Double trouble: factorization

The qubits are never entangled, so the joint state is a product state and

\[ \langle Z\otimes Z\rangle=\langle Z\otimes I\rangle\cdot\langle I\otimes Z\rangle. \]

Core theory

II.1

The formal machinery

Part I used vectors and matrices directly. Part II uses the language physicists speak: Dirac notation, operators built from outer products, and the equation for change in time.

Key points
  • A bra \(\langle b|\) is the conjugate transpose of a ket. \(\langle b|a\rangle\) is a number; \(|a\rangle\langle b|\) is a matrix.
  • \(\sum_i|i\rangle\langle i|=I\). Inserting it reads off matrix elements: \(U_{ij}=\langle i|U|j\rangle\).
  • Every gate is \(e^{i\theta G}\) for a Hermitian generator \(G\). The Paulis generate single-qubit rotations.
  • For a constant Hamiltonian, evolution for time \(t\) is the gate \(U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}\).

Dirac notation

A ket \(|a\rangle\) is a column vector of amplitudes. A bra \(\langle b|\) is its conjugate transpose (the dagger, \(\dagger\)): transpose it and flip the sign of every imaginary part.

A bra against a ket gives the inner product \(\langle b|a\rangle\), a single complex number that measures overlap. A ket against a bra gives the outer product \(|a\rangle\langle b|\), a matrix.

The completeness relation. For any orthonormal basis, \(\sum_i |i\rangle\langle i| = I\). You can insert this sum anywhere. That is how you read a matrix element off an operator: \(U_{ij} = \langle i|U|j\rangle\), and \(U = \sum_{i,j} U_{ij}\,|i\rangle\langle j|\).

Observables are Hermitian; gates are unitary

An observable is Hermitian (\(A=A^\dagger\)): real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. A gate is unitary (\(U^\dagger U = I\), so \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\)): it keeps lengths, so it keeps total probability, and it can always be undone. Measurement is the one step that cannot be undone.

The groups U(N) and SU(N)

The \(N\times N\) unitaries form a group, \(U(N)\): multiplying two gives a third, and each has an inverse. \(\mathrm{SU}(N)\) adds the condition \(\det U = 1\), which removes the global phase. A single-qubit gate is an element of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\). That is why three angles (the Rot gate of I.3) describe it completely.

Generators: every gate is an exponentiated observable

Any unitary can be written \(U = e^{i\theta G}\) with \(G\) Hermitian; \(G\) is the generator. For one qubit the generators are the Pauli matrices (\(\sigma\), sigma):

\[ RX(\theta)=e^{-i(\theta/2)\sigma_x}=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\sigma_x . \]

A function of a matrix is defined by its Taylor series, \(f(A)=\sum_n \tfrac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}A^n\). On an eigenstate it acts on the eigenvalue: \(f(A)|a\rangle = f(a)|a\rangle\).

How states change in time

The Schrödinger equation is \(i\hbar\,\tfrac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = \hat H|\psi\rangle\), where \(\hbar\) (h-bar) is \(h/2\pi\). For a constant \(\hat H\) it integrates to \(U(t)=e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}\). So a gate is evolution for a fixed time under some Hamiltonian.

In the Schrödinger picture states move and operators stay fixed. In the equivalent Heisenberg picture operators move by \(\tfrac{dO}{dt}=\tfrac1{i\hbar}[O,\hat H]\), where \([A,B]=AB-BA\) is the commutator.

The adiabatic theorem

If a Hamiltonian changes slowly enough, a system that starts in an energy eigenstate stays in the matching eigenstate (Born & Fock, 1928). Adiabatic quantum computing and quantum annealers use this: encode the answer as the ground state of a final Hamiltonian, start in an easy ground state, and change slowly.

II.2

Measurement, bases & interference

Measurement turns amplitudes into numbers. The rule is geometric, it depends on the basis, and its most important feature, interference, is why quantum computing works.

Key points
  • On the Bloch sphere, \(P(0)=\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\) and \(P(1)=\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\).
  • To measure in another basis, rotate that basis onto the computational basis, then measure.
  • Relative phase is invisible in one basis and visible in another.
  • If you do not look at the path, amplitudes add before squaring. Cross terms can cancel: interference.

The Born rule, geometrically

Write the state as \(|\psi\rangle=\cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle\). A computational-basis measurement gives \(P(0)=|\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2=\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\) and \(P(1)=\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\). States in the northern hemisphere tend to give \(|0\rangle\); the equator is exactly 50/50.

Measuring in a different basis

The Hadamard (X) basis is \(|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\); the Y basis is \(|y_\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). To measure in one of them, rotate it back onto the computational basis and measure normally (as in I.9). Antipodal points on the Bloch sphere are orthogonal states, so each basis is a pair of opposite poles.

Global and relative phase

A global factor \(e^{i\gamma}\) cancels in every probability. A phase between the components, the \(e^{i\varphi}\) above, does not appear in the computational basis but does in the X basis:

\[ P(+)=\tfrac12\,(1+\sin\theta\cos\varphi). \]

Interference: the two-slit logic

Suppose a particle can reach outcome \(c\) through two intermediate states \(b\). If you measure which route it took, the probabilities add: \(\sum_b |\langle c|b\rangle|^2|\langle b|a\rangle|^2\). If you do not, the amplitudes add first and are squared after, and the cross terms can cancel or reinforce. A quantum algorithm is an interference pattern built so that wrong answers cancel.

II.3

Multi-qubit gates & entanglement

Qubits combine through the tensor product. Two-qubit gates then create correlations that no classical system can reproduce.

Key points
  • Dimensions multiply: \(n\) qubits live in a \(2^n\)-dimensional space.
  • CNOT, CZ, controlled-U, and SWAP are the basic two-qubit gates. H then CNOT makes a Bell state.
  • Hadamards on both sides of a CNOT swap its control and target. Bernstein–Vazirani uses this.
  • A state that does not factorize is entangled.

The tensor product

Two systems join by the tensor product \(\otimes\). Two qubits live in \(\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2=\mathbb{C}^4\), with basis \(|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\). In components it is the Kronecker product. Operators follow \((A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD\): each factor acts on its own qubit. (The direct sum \(\oplus\) instead stacks spaces and adds dimensions.)

The Pauli algebra

The Paulis multiply by one rule, \(\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\,\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k\). Here \(\delta_{ij}\) (Kronecker delta) is 1 when the indices match, and \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) (Levi-Civita symbol) encodes the cyclic order \(x\to y\to z\). Paulis on the same qubit generally do not commute; Paulis on different qubits always do.

II.4

Teleportation & entanglement protocols

Entanglement is a resource. Two protocols show what it buys you, and one theorem shows what it forbids.

Key points
  • No-cloning: linearity forbids copying an unknown state.
  • Superdense coding: one qubit plus a shared Bell pair carries two classical bits.
  • Teleportation: two classical bits plus a shared Bell pair move one unknown qubit state. The original is destroyed, and nothing travels faster than light.

The no-cloning theorem

Suppose a unitary \(U\) copied any state: \(U(|a\rangle|0\rangle)=|a\rangle|a\rangle\). Feed it \(|c\rangle=(|a\rangle+|b\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Linearity forces \((|a\rangle|a\rangle+|b\rangle|b\rangle)/\sqrt2\). A true copy would be \(|c\rangle|c\rangle\), which has four terms. The two disagree, so no such \(U\) exists.

You can copy a known state, and you can spread one qubit into \(a|0\cdots0\rangle+b|1\cdots1\rangle\). You cannot make independent duplicates. This protects quantum cryptography and is why error correction needs special methods.

Superdense coding

Alice and Bob share a Bell pair. To send two bits \((a,b)\), Alice applies \(I\), \(X\), \(Z\), or \(Y\) to her half and sends her one qubit to Bob. Bob applies a CNOT and a Hadamard, measures both qubits, and reads \((a,b)\) with certainty.

Quantum teleportation

Alice holds an unknown \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) and shares a Bell pair with Bob. She applies a CNOT and a Hadamard, measures her two qubits, and sends the two bits to Bob. Bob applies \(X^{m_2}\) and then \(Z^{m_1}\), and his qubit becomes \(|\psi\rangle\).

II.5

Reversible logic & oracles

Quantum gates are unitary, so quantum computation is reversible. An ordinary function must be repackaged before a quantum computer can call it. That repackaging, plus one phase trick, drives the first algorithms.

Key points
  • Any function \(f\) becomes reversible as \((x,y)\mapsto(x,\,y\oplus f(x))\).
  • CNOT is a reversible XOR. AND needs a third bit: the Toffoli gate.
  • The oracle is \(U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle\). Algorithms are judged by how many times they call it.
  • With the target in \(|-\rangle\), the oracle writes \((-1)^{f(x)}\) into the phase: phase kickback.

Making a function reversible

A classical computation with \(n\) input bits and \(m\) output bits is a map \(f:x\mapsto f(x)\). In general you cannot run it backwards. Extend it to \(n+m\) bits:

\[ \pi_f(x,y) = (x,\; y\oplus f(x)). \]

Here \(\oplus\) is bitwise XOR (addition mod 2). Applying \(\pi_f\) twice gives \((x,y)\) back, so it is reversible. With \(y=0\) it computes \(f\): \((x,0)\mapsto(x,f(x))\).

Reversible gates

NOT is already reversible: \(X=|0\rangle\langle1|+|1\rangle\langle0|\). XOR becomes reversible as CNOT, \(|b_1\rangle|b_0\rangle\mapsto|b_1\rangle|b_1\oplus b_0\rangle\). AND is impossible to make reversible with only two bits, because three different inputs give the output 0. A third bit fixes it.

The oracle \(U_f\)

The quantum version of \(\pi_f\) is the oracle

\[ U_f\,\big(|x\rangle\otimes|y\rangle\big)=|x\rangle\otimes|y\oplus f(x)\rangle . \]

The oracle is a "black box" that you may query. An algorithm's query complexity is how many queries it needs.

Quantum parallelism and phase kickback

Feed the oracle a superposition of all inputs and it evaluates \(f\) on all of them at once: quantum parallelism. But a measurement returns only one \((x,f(x))\) pair.

The useful trick is phase kickback. Prepare the target in \(|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). If \(f(x)=0\) nothing changes; if \(f(x)=1\) the target becomes \(-|-\rangle\). Either way:

\[ U_f\big(|x\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\big)=(-1)^{f(x)}\,|x\rangle\otimes|-\rangle . \]

Now \(f\)'s value lives in a relative phase of the input register, which interference can read. The target is unchanged and can be reused.

II.6

Physical qubits & hardware

The math does not care what a qubit is made of; the machine does. Every abstract gate becomes a physical pulse on real, noisy, partly connected hardware.

Key points
  • Any clean two-level quantum system can be a qubit: spins, ions, photons, superconducting circuits.
  • A transmon is a capacitor plus a Josephson junction. Its uneven level spacing isolates \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\).
  • Transpilation rewrites circuits for a chip's native gates and connectivity, usually making them longer.
  • \(n\) qubits need \(2^n\) amplitudes to simulate; classical simulation stops near 50 qubits.

A qubit is a spin, and many other things

The cleanest physical qubit is a spin-½ particle: \(|0\rangle=|\!\uparrow\rangle\), \(|1\rangle=|\!\downarrow\rangle\). Many other two-level systems also work:

Superconducting (transmon)The two lowest levels of a superconducting circuit with a Josephson junction. The most common platform (IBM, Google).
Trapped ionsTwo electronic levels of an ion held in an electromagnetic trap. Slow but very accurate, with all-to-all connectivity (IonQ).
PhotonsThe polarization of one photon: horizontal or vertical. Low noise, but photons are hard to make interact.
Neutral atoms, NMR, quantum dots, defectsCold atoms, nuclear spins, trapped electrons, or crystal defects (NV centers in diamond). A qudit has d levels.

Inside a transmon

A transmon is a capacitor in parallel with a Josephson junction. A plain harmonic oscillator has evenly spaced levels, so a pulse tuned to \(|0\rangle\to|1\rangle\) would also drive \(|1\rangle\to|2\rangle\). The junction is nonlinear and makes the spacing uneven (anharmonic), so the lowest two levels form a clean qubit with frequency \(\omega_{01}\).

Typical frequencies are 1–10 GHz, which is a wavelength of 3–30 cm. At refrigerator temperatures of 1–10 mK, \(k_BT\ll\hbar\omega\), so heat almost never excites the qubit and it stays in \(|0\rangle\). Replacing the single junction with a SQUID (two junctions in a loop) makes the frequency tunable with magnetic flux. V.2 uses this to search for dark matter.

Topology, transpilation, and noise

A chip lets only certain qubit pairs interact. This is its coupling map (IBM's heavy-hex lattice; IonQ's all-to-all). Your circuit must be rewritten into the native gates and connectivity, with SWAPs added where needed. This is transpilation.

Every gate and readout has a fidelity below 1, and each qubit slowly loses its state to the environment (decoherence, measured by times such as \(T_1\)). Today's machines are called NISQ: Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum.

Scale check. Simulating \(n\) qubits needs \(2^n\) complex amplitudes at about 16 bytes each: ~13 qubits ≈ 128 KB, ~36 qubits ≈ 1 TB, ~50 qubits ≈ 18 PB. That wall is the reason to build the hardware. IBM's roadmap ran Falcon (27) → Eagle (127) → Osprey (433) → Condor (1121); Google's error-corrected Willow chip has 105 qubits.

Bit strings & the Walsh–Hadamard transform

The early algorithms are short proofs about sums of \(\pm1\). These identities do all of the work, so learn them once.

Key points
  • \(x\cdot y=\sum_i x_iy_i\), taken mod 2 when it is an exponent of \(-1\).
  • \(H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle\).
  • \(\sum_x(-1)^{x\cdot y}\) is \(2^n\) if \(y=0\), and 0 for every other \(y\). This is why the final Hadamards in an algorithm concentrate the amplitude.

Vocabulary

  • An \(n\)-bit string is \(x=x_1x_2\cdots x_n\) with each \(x_i\in\{0,1\}\). There are \(N=2^n\) of them.
  • The Hamming weight \(|x|\) is the number of 1s. The Hamming distance between \(x\) and \(y\) is \(|x\oplus y|\), the number of positions where they differ.
  • The bitwise dot product is \(x\cdot y=\sum_i x_iy_i\). It equals \(|x\wedge y|\), the number of positions where both strings are 1.

Identities you will use

\[ x\cdot y \bmod 2 = \frac{1-(-1)^{x\cdot y}}{2},\qquad (x\cdot y)\oplus(x\cdot z)=x\cdot(y\oplus z)\pmod 2 \] \[ |x\oplus y| \equiv |x|+|y| \pmod 2,\qquad \sum_{x}(-1)^{x\cdot x} = 0\quad(n\ge1) \]

The most important one is the orthogonality sum. The sum over \(x\) factorizes into one factor per bit:

\[ \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(1+(-1)^{y_i}\big)=\begin{cases}2^n & y=0\cdots0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

If any bit \(y_i=1\), its factor is \(1-1=0\), and the whole product is 0.

The Walsh–Hadamard transform

On one qubit, \(H|x\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{z}(-1)^{xz}|z\rangle\). Apply it to every qubit and the signs multiply:

\[ W|x\rangle = H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{y}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle,\qquad H^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x,y}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle\langle x|. \]

With \(x=0\cdots0\) every sign is \(+1\), so \(W\) makes the uniform superposition. \(W\) is its own inverse, so applying it to a sign pattern \(\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle\) gives back \(|x\rangle\).

Worked example

\(H\otimes H\) on \(|10\rangle\)

  1. The signs are \((-1)^{10\cdot y}\) for \(y=00,01,10,11\). The dot products are \(0,0,1,1\).
  2. So \(H^{\otimes2}|10\rangle=\tfrac12\big(|00\rangle+|01\rangle-|10\rangle-|11\rangle\big)\).
  3. Check: this equals \(|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\), which is \(H|1\rangle\otimes H|0\rangle\).

Deutsch & Deutsch–Jozsa

The first proofs that a quantum computer can beat a classical one. Both learn a global property of a function with a single oracle call.

Key points
  • Recipe: Hadamards, one oracle call with the target in \(|-\rangle\), Hadamards, measure.
  • Deutsch decides whether \(f(0)=f(1)\), which is the parity \(f(0)\oplus f(1)\), with one query instead of two.
  • Deutsch–Jozsa: if \(f\) is constant you measure \(0\cdots0\) with probability 1; if balanced, with probability 0.
  • A deterministic classical algorithm can need \(2^{n-1}+1\) queries. A randomized one with a small error needs only a few, so the separation is for exact answers.

Deutsch's problem

A function \(f:\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}\) is constant (\(f(0)=f(1)\)) or balanced (\(f(0)\ne f(1)\)). Classically you must evaluate it twice. Deutsch (1985) showed that one quantum query is enough.

|0> --[H]--[   ]--[H]--[M]     measure 0  =>  constant
           [ Uf]              measure 1  =>  balanced
|1> --[H]--[   ]--------
Step by step

Why one query works

  1. Start in \(|0\rangle|1\rangle\). Hadamards give \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\otimes|-\rangle\).
  2. Phase kickback (II.5) turns the oracle into a phase: \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big((-1)^{f(0)}|0\rangle+(-1)^{f(1)}|1\rangle\big)\otimes|-\rangle\).
  3. Factor out \((-1)^{f(0)}\). The input qubit is \(|+\rangle\) if \(f(0)=f(1)\) and \(|-\rangle\) if not (up to a global sign).
  4. The last Hadamard maps \(|+\rangle\to|0\rangle\) and \(|-\rangle\to|1\rangle\). The measurement gives \(f(0)\oplus f(1)\) with certainty.

Deutsch–Jozsa

Now \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}\) is promised to be either constant or balanced (0 on exactly half of the inputs). The circuit is the same with \(n\) input qubits:

\[ |0\rangle^{\otimes n}|1\rangle\ \xrightarrow{H^{\otimes(n+1)}}\ \frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_x|x\rangle|-\rangle\ \xrightarrow{U_f}\ \frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle\ \xrightarrow{H^{\otimes n}}\ \frac1N\sum_y\Big[\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)+x\cdot y}\Big]|y\rangle|-\rangle . \]

Look at the amplitude of \(y=0\cdots0\). It is \(\tfrac1N\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}\).

  • Constant: every term is the same sign, so the amplitude is \(\pm1\). You measure \(0\cdots0\) every time.
  • Balanced: half the terms are \(+1\) and half are \(-1\), so the amplitude is 0. You never measure \(0\cdots0\).
The ancilla is reusable. The target qubit leaves the oracle as \(|-\rangle\), unchanged and unentangled. It can serve the next oracle call.
What this does not show. These algorithms do not compute \(f\) faster. They reduce the number of questions asked of a black box. That query-complexity view is where quantum advantage is first proven.

Bernstein–Vazirani & Simon

Bernstein–Vazirani reads a whole hidden string in one query. Simon finds a hidden XOR period exponentially faster than any classical method, and is the direct ancestor of Shor's algorithm.

Key points
  • BV: the oracle phase pattern \((-1)^{s\cdot x}\) is exactly \(H^{\otimes n}|s\rangle\), so the final Hadamards return \(|s\rangle\).
  • Simon: each run gives a random \(y\) with \(y\cdot a=0 \pmod 2\). About \(n\) runs give enough linear equations to solve for \(a\).
  • Simon needs \(O(n)\) queries plus polynomial classical work; classical algorithms need \(\Omega(2^{n/2})\) queries.

Bernstein–Vazirani

The oracle computes \(f(x)=s\cdot x \bmod 2\) for an unknown string \(s\). Classically each query reveals one bit of \(s\) (query \(x=0\cdots010\cdots0\)), so you need \(n\) queries. The quantum circuit is the Deutsch–Jozsa circuit. After the oracle the input register holds

\[ |\psi_s\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_x(-1)^{s\cdot x}|x\rangle = H^{\otimes n}|s\rangle . \]

The states \(|\psi_s\rangle\) for different \(s\) are orthonormal, so one more \(H^{\otimes n}\) maps \(|\psi_s\rangle\) to \(|s\rangle\) and the measurement reads \(s\) with certainty.

The CNOT picture

Simon's problem

Now \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^n\) and there is a hidden nonzero string \(a\) such that \(f(x)=f(y)\) exactly when \(y=x\) or \(y=x\oplus a\). The function is two-to-one with an XOR period \(a\). Find \(a\).

  1. Apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) to the first register and query the oracle into a second \(n\)-qubit register.
  2. Measure the second register. It gives some \(f(x_0)\), and the first register collapses to \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|x_0\rangle+|x_0\oplus a\rangle)\).
  3. Apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) again. The amplitude of \(|y\rangle\) is proportional to \((-1)^{x_0\cdot y}\big(1+(-1)^{a\cdot y}\big)\), which is zero unless \(a\cdot y=0\pmod 2\).
  4. Measure a random \(y\) with \(y\cdot a=0\). Repeat until you have \(n-1\) linearly independent \(y\)s, then solve the linear system mod 2 by Gaussian elimination.
Worked example

Recover \(a\) for \(n=3\)

  1. Suppose \(a=101\). The outcomes with \(y\cdot a=0\) are \(000,\ 010,\ 101,\ 111\), each with probability \(\tfrac14\).
  2. You measure \(y=010\): the equation is \(a_2=0\).
  3. You measure \(y=101\): the equation is \(a_1\oplus a_3=0\), so \(a_1=a_3\).
  4. The only nonzero solution is \(a=101\). Two independent equations (\(n-1=2\)) were enough.

A classical algorithm must find a collision \(f(x)=f(y)\). By the birthday bound that takes about \(2^{n/2}\) queries. Simon's algorithm needs \(O(n)\) queries, an exponential separation. Shor's algorithm (III.10) uses the same idea with an ordinary period instead of an XOR period.

Measuring overlaps: swap, Hadamard & inversion tests

Many algorithms, and most quantum classifiers, need the overlap \(\langle a|b\rangle\) of two states. Three small circuits estimate it from the statistics of one ancilla qubit.

Key points
  • Swap test: \(P(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12|\langle a|b\rangle|^2\). It gives the size of the overlap, not its sign.
  • Hadamard test: \(P(0)=\tfrac12\big(1+\mathrm{Re}\langle a|b\rangle\big)\). An extra phase gate gives the imaginary part.
  • Inversion test: prepare \(|a\rangle\), undo the preparation of \(|b\rangle\), and count all-zero outcomes.
  • Each run gives one bit. Precision \(\varepsilon\) needs about \(1/\varepsilon^2\) runs.

The swap test

|0>  --[H]----●----[H]--[M]
              |
|a>  -------[SWAP]---------
|b>  -------[SWAP]---------

After the first H the state is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle|a\rangle|b\rangle+|1\rangle|a\rangle|b\rangle)\). The controlled SWAP changes the second branch to \(|1\rangle|b\rangle|a\rangle\). The last H interferes the two branches:

\[ P(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12|\langle a|b\rangle|^2,\qquad |\langle a|b\rangle|^2=2P(0)-1 . \]

Identical states give \(P(0)=1\); orthogonal states give \(P(0)=\tfrac12\). For two \(n\)-qubit states the circuit uses \(2n+1\) qubits.

Recovering the sign

For real unit vectors, \(|\langle a|b\rangle|^2\) cannot tell \(a^{\mathsf T}b=0.5\) from \(-0.5\). Fix it by adding one component before encoding: \(a'=(a,1)/\sqrt2\) and \(b'=(b,1)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\langle a'|b'\rangle=(a^{\mathsf T}b+1)/2\ge0\), so the square root is safe:

\[ a^{\mathsf T}b = 2\sqrt{2P(0)-1}\;-\;1 . \]

The Hadamard test

Prepare \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle|a\rangle+|1\rangle|b\rangle\big)\), apply H to the ancilla, and measure it:

\[ P(0)=\tfrac12\big(1+\mathrm{Re}\,\langle a|b\rangle\big). \]

Insert a phase gate (S or S†) on the ancilla before the last H to get \(\mathrm{Im}\langle a|b\rangle\) instead. To prepare the input from two circuits \(U_a|0\rangle=|a\rangle\) and \(U_b|0\rangle=|b\rangle\): apply H to the ancilla, apply \(U_b\) to the data, apply \(U_aU_b^\dagger\) controlled on the ancilla, then apply X to the ancilla.

The inversion test

Apply \(U_a\), then \(U_b^\dagger\), and measure. The probability of the all-zero outcome is

\[ P(0\cdots0)=|\langle0|U_b^\dagger U_a|0\rangle|^2=|\langle b|a\rangle|^2 . \]

No ancilla and no second register, but you must know how to build \(|b\rangle\) and run its inverse.

Read the cost carefully. A classical inner product of two \(N\)-vectors costs \(O(N)\). The swap test uses only \(O(\log N)\) qubits, but it needs the states already loaded, and loading an arbitrary vector can itself cost \(O(N)\). The repetitions also scale as \(1/\varepsilon^2\).

The discrete & fast Fourier transforms

Before the quantum version, the classical one. The DFT changes a list of samples into a list of frequencies. The FFT does it in \(O(N\log N)\) steps instead of \(O(N^2)\).

Key points
  • \(\tilde f_j=\tfrac1{\sqrt N}\sum_k w^{-jk}f_k\) with \(w=e^{2\pi i/N}\). With this normalization the DFT is unitary.
  • The FFT splits the sum into even and odd terms and uses \(w^{k+N/2}=-w^k\). Cost: \(O(N\log N)\).
  • Convolution becomes multiplication. That is how the FFT multiplies polynomials fast.

The DFT

Take \(N\) samples \(f_0,\dots,f_{N-1}\). The discrete Fourier transform is

\[ \tilde f_j=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} w^{-jk} f_k,\qquad w=e^{2\pi i/N}. \]

\(w\) is a primitive \(N\)-th root of unity: \(w^N=1\). The matrix with entries \(w^{-jk}/\sqrt N\) is unitary, so the inverse uses \(w^{+jk}\). Computing every \(\tilde f_j\) directly takes \(N\) multiplications each, so \(N^2\) in total.

The FFT (Cooley–Tukey, 1965)

Let \(N\) be a power of 2. Split the sum into even and odd \(k\). Each half is a DFT of size \(N/2\), called \(E_j\) and \(O_j\):

\[ \tilde f_j=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(E_j+w^{-j}O_j\big),\qquad \tilde f_{j+N/2}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(E_j-w^{-j}O_j\big). \]

The second line uses \(w^{N/2}=-1\): the two halves of the output share the same \(E_j\) and \(O_j\). Repeat the split \(\log_2N\) times. Each level costs \(O(N)\), so the total is \(O(N\log N)\).

Convolution and polynomial multiplication

The convolution theorem: the DFT of a convolution \((f*g)_m=\sum_k f_kg_{m-k}\) is the pointwise product of the DFTs (up to a normalization factor). Multiplying two polynomials is a convolution of their coefficient lists, so:

  1. Pad both coefficient lists with zeros to a power of 2 at least as long as the product.
  2. FFT both lists. This evaluates each polynomial at the \(N\)-th roots of unity.
  3. Multiply the values pointwise.
  4. Inverse FFT the result to get the product's coefficients.

The product has \(3+2-1=4\) coefficients, already a power of 2. The FFT method returns \([4,13,22,15]\), which is \(4+13x+22x^2+15x^3\).

The quantum Fourier transform

The QFT is the DFT applied to the \(2^n\) amplitudes of an \(n\)-qubit state. It takes \(O(n^2)\) gates, exponentially fewer than the FFT's \(O(N\log N)\) operations, but the result is a quantum state that you cannot read out directly.

Key points
  • \(F|j\rangle=2^{-n/2}\sum_k e^{2\pi i jk/2^n}|k\rangle\). Note the \(+\) sign, opposite to the DFT above.
  • The output is a product state. Each qubit gets a phase set by a binary fraction of \(j\).
  • Circuit: on each qubit, a Hadamard and then controlled \(R_k\) rotations; finally reverse the qubit order with SWAPs.
  • \(n(n+1)/2\) gates plus \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) SWAPs. Useful only inside a larger algorithm, such as phase estimation.

Definition

\[ F|j\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{k=0}^{2^n-1}e^{2\pi i\,jk/2^n}|k\rangle . \]

For one qubit, \(F=H\). For two qubits, with \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/4}=i\):

\[ F_4=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&i&-1&-i\\1&-1&1&-1\\1&-i&-1&i\end{pmatrix}. \]
Sign convention. The DFT in III.5 uses \(w^{-jk}\); the QFT uses \(e^{+2\pi ijk/N}\). The two differ only by a sign in the exponent. Check which convention a library uses.

The product form

Write \(j=j_1j_2\cdots j_n\) in binary and use binary fractions \(0.j_\ell j_{\ell+1}\cdots j_n=j_\ell/2+j_{\ell+1}/4+\cdots\). The sum over \(k\) factorizes:

\[ F|j_1\cdots j_n\rangle=\frac{\big(|0\rangle+e^{2\pi i\,0.j_n}|1\rangle\big)\big(|0\rangle+e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-1}j_n}|1\rangle\big)\cdots\big(|0\rangle+e^{2\pi i\,0.j_1j_2\cdots j_n}|1\rangle\big)}{2^{n/2}} . \]

So the QFT of a basis state is not entangled. Each output qubit is \(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\), and the circuit only has to build the right phases.

The circuit

Use the phase rotation \(R_k=\mathrm{diag}\big(1,\,e^{2\pi i/2^k}\big)\). Note \(R_1=Z\), \(R_2=S\), \(R_3=T\). For three qubits:

q1 --[H]--[R2]--[R3]--------------------x--
           |     |                      |
q2 --------●-----|-----[H]--[R2]--------|--
                 |           |          |
q3 --------------●-----------●----[H]---x--
  1. Qubit 1: H, then \(R_2\) controlled by qubit 2, then \(R_3\) controlled by qubit 3. It now holds the phase \(0.j_1j_2j_3\).
  2. Qubit 2: H, then \(R_2\) controlled by qubit 3. Phase \(0.j_2j_3\).
  3. Qubit 3: H. Phase \(0.j_3\).
  4. The factors come out in reverse order, so SWAP qubits 1 and 3.

For \(n\) qubits that is \(n\) Hadamards and \(n(n-1)/2\) controlled rotations, \(n(n+1)/2\) gates in total, plus \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) SWAPs. So the QFT costs \(O(n^2)=O\big((\log N)^2\big)\) gates.

Why it does not replace the FFT

The FFT gives you all \(N\) numbers. The QFT gives you a state whose amplitudes are those numbers, and a measurement returns one index \(k\) with probability \(|\tilde f_k|^2\). Loading the input is also costly. The QFT is useful when the input comes from another quantum step and you only need a global feature, such as a period. Phase estimation and Shor's algorithm use it that way.

Quantum phase estimation

Given a unitary \(U\) and one of its eigenvectors \(|u\rangle\), QPE finds the phase \(\theta\) in \(U|u\rangle=e^{2\pi i\theta}|u\rangle\). It is the core subroutine of Shor's algorithm, quantum counting, and quantum chemistry.

Key points
  • \(n\) counting qubits in \(|+\rangle\), controlled-\(U^{2^j}\) gates, then the inverse QFT.
  • Phase kickback writes \(e^{2\pi i\theta k}\) onto counting state \(|k\rangle\). That is the QFT of \(|2^n\theta\rangle\), so QFT† reads \(\theta\) as an \(n\)-bit binary fraction.
  • Exact if \(\theta=a/2^n\). Otherwise the best \(n\)-bit estimate appears with probability at least \(4/\pi^2\approx0.405\).
  • Precision \(\varepsilon\) needs \(O(\log 1/\varepsilon)\) qubits but \(O(1/\varepsilon)\) uses of controlled-\(U\). QPE is not a fast way to get many digits.

The circuit

|0> --[H]-------------------------●------[      ]--[M]
|0> --[H]---------------●---------|------[ QFT† ]--[M]
|0> --[H]------●--------|---------|------[      ]--[M]
               |        |         |
|u> ----------[U]------[U²]------[U⁴]-----------------  |u>  (unchanged)
  1. Hadamard every counting qubit.
  2. Counting qubit \(j\) controls \(U^{2^j}\). Because \(|u\rangle\) is an eigenvector, each controlled gate only kicks back the phase \(e^{2\pi i\theta 2^j}\). The counting register becomes \(2^{-n/2}\sum_k e^{2\pi i\theta k}|k\rangle\).
  3. That is exactly \(F|2^n\theta\rangle\) when \(2^n\theta\) is an integer. Apply \(F^\dagger\) and measure an integer \(m\). The estimate is \(\theta\approx m/2^n\).

Cost: \(n\) Hadamards, \(2^n-1\) controlled-\(U\) applications (if \(U^{2^j}\) is built by repetition), and \(O(n^2)\) gates for QFT†. Shor's algorithm is fast because its \(U^{2^j}\) can be built directly by repeated squaring (III.8).

Modular arithmetic for Shor

Shor's algorithm is mostly number theory, with one quantum step. This section collects the classical tools: periods, fast powers, inverses, and the gcd.

Key points
  • The order \(r\) of \(a\) mod \(N\) is the smallest \(r>0\) with \(a^r\equiv1\). The sequence \(a^x \bmod N\) repeats with period \(r\).
  • Repeated squaring computes \(a^x\bmod N\) in \(O(\log x)\) multiplications.
  • Euclid's algorithm finds \(\gcd(a,b)\) in \(O(\log\min(a,b))\) steps.
  • \(a\) has an inverse mod \(N\) exactly when \(\gcd(a,N)=1\).

Congruence and periods

\(a\equiv b\pmod N\) means \(N\) divides \(a-b\). Powers mod \(N\) repeat:

x01234Period
2^x mod 7124123
3^x mod 10139714

Fast powers by repeated squaring

Worked example

\(91^{43}\bmod131\)

  1. Write the exponent in binary: \(43=101011_2=32+8+2+1\).
  2. Square repeatedly mod 131: \(91^1=91\), \(91^2\equiv28\), \(91^4\equiv129\), \(91^8\equiv4\), \(91^{16}\equiv16\), \(91^{32}\equiv125\).
  3. Multiply the powers for the 1 bits: \(91^{43}\equiv125\cdot4\cdot28\cdot91\pmod{131}\).
  4. Reduce after each product: \(125\cdot4\equiv107\), \(107\cdot28\equiv114\), \(114\cdot91\equiv\mathbf{25}\).

Six squarings and three multiplications instead of 42 multiplications.

Inverses, Fermat, and Euler

The modular inverse of \(a\) is \(a^{-1}\) with \(a\cdot a^{-1}\equiv1\pmod N\). For example \(3\cdot7=21\equiv1\pmod{10}\), so \(3^{-1}\equiv7\). It exists exactly when \(\gcd(a,N)=1\).

Fermat's little theorem: for a prime \(p\) that does not divide \(a\), \(a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\). Euler's theorem generalizes it: \(a^{\varphi(N)}\equiv1\pmod N\) when \(\gcd(a,N)=1\), where \(\varphi(N)\) counts the numbers below \(N\) that are coprime to \(N\). For \(N=pq\), \(\varphi(N)=(p-1)(q-1)\). RSA depends on this.

Euclid's algorithm

\(\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(b,\ a\bmod b)\). Repeat until the remainder is 0:

\[ 72=3\cdot20+12,\quad 20=1\cdot12+8,\quad 12=1\cdot8+4,\quad 8=2\cdot4+0\ \Rightarrow\ \gcd(72,20)=4. \]

Run backwards (the extended Euclidean algorithm), it also finds modular inverses.

Factoring & RSA

RSA encryption is safe only while factoring is hard. Factoring reduces to finding the order of a number mod \(N\), and that is the one step a quantum computer does fast.

Key points
  • If \(r\) (the order of \(a\)) is even and \(a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N\), then \(\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1,\,N)\) are factors of \(N\).
  • For a random \(a\) (with \(N\) odd and not a prime power), these conditions hold with probability at least \(\tfrac12\).
  • RSA: public key \((N,c)\), private key \(d\) with \(cd\equiv1\pmod{\varphi(N)}\). Encrypt \(b=a^c\bmod N\); decrypt \(a=b^d\bmod N\).
  • Anyone who can factor \(N\) can compute \(\varphi(N)\) and then \(d\).

From order to factors

Suppose \(a^r\equiv1\pmod N\) with \(r\) even. Then \(N\) divides \(a^r-1=(a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)\). \(N\) does not divide \(a^{r/2}-1\) (otherwise the order would be \(r/2\)). If it also does not divide \(a^{r/2}+1\), each factor shares a nontrivial divisor with \(N\), and Euclid's algorithm finds it.

Worked example

Factor \(N=91\) with \(a=4\)

  1. Powers of 4 mod 91: \(4,\ 16,\ 64,\ 74,\ 23,\ 1\). The order is \(r=6\), which is even.
  2. \(a^{r/2}=4^3=64\). It is not \(\equiv-1=90\), so the method works.
  3. \(\gcd(64+1,91)=\gcd(65,91)=13\) and \(\gcd(64-1,91)=\gcd(63,91)=7\).
  4. Check: \(7\times13=91\).
Worked example

Factor \(N=15\) with \(a=13\)

  1. Powers of 13 mod 15: \(13,\ 4,\ 7,\ 1\). The order is \(r=4\).
  2. \(13^2=169\). \(\gcd(168,15)=3\) and \(\gcd(170,15)=5\).

Not every base is this short: \(19^x\bmod 91\) has period 12.

Shor's algorithm

Classical steps around one quantum subroutine: order finding. The quantum part is phase estimation for the "multiply by \(a\)" operator, and it runs in polynomial time.

Key points
  • Order finding = QPE on \(U|y\rangle=|ay\bmod N\rangle\). Its eigenphases are \(s/r\).
  • The QFT turns a periodic state into peaks at multiples of \(Q/r\). Continued fractions recover \(r\).
  • Quantum cost \(O\big((\log N)^2\log\log N\,\log\log\log N\big)\) versus the classical number field sieve, \(\exp\big(1.9(\log N)^{1/3}(\log\log N)^{2/3}\big)\).

The five steps

  1. If \(N\) is even, return 2. (Also check that \(N\) is not a prime power; that is classically easy.)
  2. Pick a random \(a\) with \(1<a<N\). If \(\gcd(a,N)>1\), you found a factor by luck.
  3. Quantum: find the order \(r\) of \(a\) mod \(N\).
  4. If \(r\) is odd or \(a^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod N\), go back to step 2.
  5. Return \(\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,N)\) and \(\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,N)\).

The order-finding circuit

Use a source register of \(K\) qubits (\(Q=2^K\), with \(N^2\le Q<2N^2\)) and a target register of \(n=\lceil\log_2N\rceil\) qubits.

  1. \(H^{\otimes K}\) on the source: \(\tfrac1{\sqrt Q}\sum_q|q\rangle|0\rangle\).
  2. Modular exponentiation \(U_a|q\rangle|0\rangle=|q\rangle|a^q\bmod N\rangle\), built by repeated squaring.
  3. Measure the target. It shows some value \(a^{q_0}\), and the source collapses to the periodic state \(\sum_j|q_0+jr\rangle\).
  4. Apply the QFT to the source. The amplitude concentrates near \(c\approx kQ/r\) for integers \(k\).
  5. Measure \(c\). Expand \(c/Q\) as a continued fraction to get \(k/r\) in lowest terms. If \(\gcd(k,r)=1\) the denominator is \(r\); otherwise repeat. Repeating \(O(\log N)\) times makes failure unlikely.
Worked example

Order finding for \(N=15,\ a=13\) with \(Q=16\)

  1. The order is \(r=4\), so the QFT peaks are exactly at multiples of \(Q/r=4\): \(c\in\{0,4,8,12\}\), each with probability \(\tfrac14\).
  2. \(c=0\): \(c/Q=0\). No information.
  3. \(c=4\): \(4/16=1/4\), so \(r=4\). Correct.
  4. \(c=8\): \(8/16=1/2\), so \(r=2\). Wrong (\(13^2\equiv4\ne1\)), because \(\gcd(k,r)=\gcd(2,4)\ne1\). The check rejects it.
  5. \(c=12\): \(12/16=3/4\), so \(r=4\). Correct.

Each run succeeds with probability \(\tfrac12\).

Why it is phase estimation

Define \(U|y\rangle=|ay\bmod N\rangle\). Its eigenvectors are

\[ |u_s\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi i sk/r}\,|a^k\bmod N\rangle,\qquad U|u_s\rangle=e^{2\pi i s/r}|u_s\rangle . \]

You cannot prepare one \(|u_s\rangle\) without knowing \(r\). But their uniform sum is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt r}\sum_s|u_s\rangle=|1\rangle\), which is easy. Run QPE on \(|1\rangle\) and you get \(s/r\) for a random \(s\). The controlled \(U^{2^j}\) gates are fast because \(a^{2^j}\bmod N\) comes from repeated squaring.

Discrete logarithms & hidden subgroups

Shor's method also breaks the discrete logarithm, the basis of Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography. Simon, factoring, and discrete log are all one problem: the hidden subgroup problem.

Key points
  • Discrete log: given \(b\), \(y\), \(p\), find \(x\) with \(b^x\equiv y\pmod p\).
  • Shor's DLP algorithm finds the hidden subgroup \(\{(mx,m)\}\) of \(f(g,h)=b^{-g}y^h\).
  • For abelian (commutative) groups the HSP has an efficient quantum algorithm. For general non-abelian groups it is open.

Groups in one paragraph

A group is a set with an operation that is closed, associative, has an identity element, and gives every element an inverse. Examples: the integers mod \(N\) under addition, \(\mathbb Z_N\); and \(\mathbb Z_p^*=\{1,\dots,p-1\}\) under multiplication mod a prime \(p\). A group is abelian if the operation commutes.

The discrete logarithm

Worked example

Solve \(2^x\equiv8\pmod{11}\)

  1. List powers of 2 mod 11 for \(x=1,\dots,10\): \(2,4,8,5,10,9,7,3,6,1\).
  2. 8 appears at \(x=3\).
  3. Every nonzero residue appears once, so 2 is a generator of \(\mathbb Z_{11}^*\).

For a prime with hundreds of digits, listing is impossible, and no efficient classical method is known.

Shor's trick: with \(y=b^x\), define \(f(g,h)=b^{-g}y^h=b^{xh-g}\). The value depends only on \(xh-g\), so \(f(g+mx,\ h+m)=f(g,h)\) for all \(m\). The set \(\{(mx,m)\}\) is a hidden subgroup of \(\mathbb Z_{p-1}\times\mathbb Z_{p-1}\). A two-register QFT reveals it, and \(x\) follows.

Grover's search

Find the one marked item among \(N\) with about \(\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N\) oracle calls instead of \(N/2\). The speedup is only quadratic, but it applies to almost any search.

Key points
  • Oracle \(O=I-2|a\rangle\langle a|\) flips the sign of the marked state. Diffusion \(S=2|\psi_0\rangle\langle\psi_0|-I\) reflects about the uniform superposition.
  • \(G=SO\) rotates the state by \(2\theta_0\) toward \(|a\rangle\), where \(\sin\theta_0=1/\sqrt N\).
  • After \(m\) steps \(P=\sin^2\big((2m+1)\theta_0\big)\). Stop at \(m\approx\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N\); more steps make it worse.
  • With \(M\) marked items, \(m\approx\tfrac\pi4\sqrt{N/M}\).

The problem

A sorted phone book is searched by bisection in \(\log_2N\) steps. Searching it by phone number (an unsorted search) takes \(N/2\) looks on average. For \(N=10^6\): 20 looks sorted, 500,000 unsorted. Grover needs about 785 oracle calls, and then succeeds with probability \(\approx0.99999996\).

The oracle

With an ancilla in \(|-\rangle\), phase kickback gives \(O|x\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle\), where \(f(x)=1\) only for \(x=a\). So \(O=I-2|a\rangle\langle a|\). To build it for \(a=01001\): apply X to the qubits where \(a\) has a 0, apply a multi-controlled NOT onto the ancilla, and undo the X gates. PennyLane's control_values does the X wrapping for you:

def oracle(combo):                       # Codebook G.3.1
    qp.MultiControlledX(wires=query_register + aux, control_values=combo)

def diffusion():                         # Codebook G.3.2
    hadamard_transform(query_register)
    qp.MultiControlledX(wires=query_register + aux,
                        control_values=[0] * len(query_register))
    hadamard_transform(query_register)

The geometry

Everything happens in the plane spanned by \(|a\rangle\) and \(|a^\perp\rangle\) (the uniform superposition of the unmarked states). The start state is

\[ |\psi_0\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle=\sin\theta_0\,|a\rangle+\cos\theta_0\,|a^\perp\rangle,\qquad \sin\theta_0=\frac1{\sqrt N}. \]

\(O\) reflects about \(|a^\perp\rangle\). \(S\) reflects about \(|\psi_0\rangle\). Two reflections make a rotation by twice the angle between the mirrors, so each Grover iteration \(G=SO\) turns the state by \(2\theta_0\) toward \(|a\rangle\):

\[ G^m|\psi_0\rangle=\sin\big((2m+1)\theta_0\big)|a\rangle+\cos\big((2m+1)\theta_0\big)|a^\perp\rangle,\qquad P_m=\sin^2\big((2m+1)\theta_0\big). \]

The best \(m\) makes \((2m+1)\theta_0\approx\pi/2\), so \(m\approx\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N\). \(P>\tfrac12\) whenever \(\tfrac\pi8\sqrt N\lesssim m\lesssim\tfrac{3\pi}8\sqrt N\). Past the peak the state rotates away and \(P\) falls again.

On real hardware

Figgatt et al. (Nature Communications, 2017) ran 3-qubit Grover on trapped ions. With one marked item out of \(N=8\), one iteration succeeds with probability \(\sin^2(3\theta_0)=78.125\%\) in theory, against 25% for a classical algorithm with one query and one guess. With two marked items, one iteration succeeds with 100%, against 46.4% classically.

Many solutions and counting

With \(M\) marked items, \(\sin\theta_0=\sqrt{M/N}\) and \(m\approx\tfrac\pi4\sqrt{N/M}\). If \(M\) is unknown, quantum counting finds it: the eigenvalues of \(G\) are \(e^{\pm2i\theta_0}\), so phase estimation on \(G\) (III.7) gives \(\theta_0\) and then \(M\).

Uses and limits

  • Uses: finding hash preimages (it halves the effective key length of symmetric cryptography), Grover adaptive search for optimization, the Dürr–Høyer minimum-finding algorithm, and as a subroutine wherever a search is the bottleneck (amplitude amplification).
  • Limits: the oracle must be a circuit; searching real data needs QRAM, which does not exist at scale. The circuit depth grows as \(\sqrt N\), and noise accumulates. A quadratic speedup is easily lost to slower quantum gate times.

The lectures also point to Kwon & Bae (Phys. Rev. A, 2021) on quantum amplitude-amplification operators.

Part IV

Quantum machine learning

How classical data gets into a quantum computer, the classical models that quantum methods borrow from, and a complete classifier built from one Hadamard gate.

IV.1Lectures 9/11 – 9/23

Data encoding & variational circuits

To learn from classical data on a quantum computer, you must first load the data into a quantum state. The encoding decides what the model can do, and how much it costs.

Key points
  • Pipeline: encode the data, process it with a parametrized circuit, measure.
  • Basis, amplitude, angle, time-evolution, and Hamiltonian encodings trade qubits against circuit depth.
  • Data is usually standardized and normalized first, because quantum states are unit vectors.
  • Variational circuits are trained by a classical optimizer in a hybrid loop.

The pipeline

Quantum machine learning follows the classical loop, dataset in and prediction out, but the middle is a quantum circuit. Encode the data into a state, process it with a parametrized unitary, then measure to read a prediction. The first step, the feature map, carries classical numbers into Hilbert space.

Five ways to encode data

Basis encodingA bit string x becomes the basis state |x⟩. Simple; n qubits for n bits.
Amplitude encodingA normalized vector of 2ⁿ numbers is stored in the amplitudes, |ψ⟩ = Σ xᵢ|i⟩. Exponentially compact, but preparing it can be expensive.
Angle (rotation) encodingEach value becomes a rotation angle, for example RY(xⱼ) on qubit j. One number per qubit.
Time-evolution encodingA value x sets the evolution time: U(x) = e^{−ixH}.
Hamiltonian encodingA matrix A is loaded as a Hamiltonian (block-embedded if it is not Hermitian).

Preprocessing

Before encoding, data is usually standardized (zero mean, unit variance) and normalized (scaled to unit length, so points lie on a circle or sphere). Amplitude encoding requires the second step. The classic example is Fisher's Iris flower dataset.

A unit vector in \(n\) dimensions can also be stored as \(n-1\) angles, using hyperspherical coordinates:

\[ x_1=\cos\varphi_1,\quad x_2=\sin\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2,\quad\dots,\quad x_n=\sin\varphi_1\cdots\sin\varphi_{n-1}. \]

For \(n=2\) this is one angle on the unit circle, which one RY rotation can load.

Data re-uploading & variational circuits

One qubit alone is a weak model, since a gate only rotates one Bloch sphere. If you re-upload the same data several times, between trainable rotations, the layers act like the hidden layers of a neural network, and even one qubit becomes a universal classifier.

More generally, a variational quantum algorithm (VQA) runs a parametrized circuit \(U(x;\theta)\) and measures a cost such as \(\mathbb{E}[(y-f(x;\theta))^2]\). A classical optimizer then updates \(\theta\). This hybrid loop is also how the VQE (variational quantum eigensolver) finds ground-state energies.

IV.2Lecture 10/7

Support vector machines & kernels

A classical classifier that maps well onto quantum hardware. The kernel trick replaces a feature map with inner products, and inner products are what quantum circuits measure.

Key points
  • A linear classifier is \(h(x)=\mathrm{sign}(w\cdot x+b)\). The SVM picks the \(w\) with the widest margin.
  • Training is \(\min\|w\|^2\) subject to \(y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge1\). Only the support vectors matter.
  • In the dual form the data appears only as inner products, so a kernel \(k(x,x')=\varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(x')\) can replace them.
  • A quantum kernel is \(\kappa(x,x')=|\langle\varphi(x')|\varphi(x)\rangle|^2\), estimated with an overlap test.

Maximum margin

Training data are pairs \((x_i,y_i)\) with labels \(y_i=\pm1\). A hyperplane \(w\cdot x+b=0\) separates them if every point is on its correct side. The margin is the distance from the hyperplane to the closest point:

\[ \gamma=\min_i\frac{|w\cdot x_i+b|}{\|w\|}. \]

Scaling \(w\) and \(b\) by the same factor does not move the hyperplane, so fix the scale by requiring \(y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge1\), with equality for the closest points. Then \(\gamma=1/\|w\|\), and maximizing the margin means

\[ \min_{w,b}\ \|w\|^2\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge1\ \text{for all } i. \]

The points with equality are the support vectors. The solution depends only on them.

Soft margin

Real data is rarely separable. Add a slack \(\xi_i\ge0\) per point and a penalty \(C\):

\[ \min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge1-\xi_i . \]

This is the same as minimizing the hinge loss \(\max(0,\,1-y_i(w\cdot x_i+b))\) plus a size penalty. Large \(C\) punishes errors; small \(C\) prefers a wide margin.

The dual form and the kernel trick

The optimization can be rewritten in terms of one weight \(\alpha_i\) per training point, with \(0\le\alpha_i\le C\) and \(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i=0\). The classifier becomes

\[ h(x)=\mathrm{sign}\Big(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,k(x_i,x)+b\Big),\qquad K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)=\varphi(x_i)\cdot\varphi(x_j). \]

The data appears only through the kernel \(k\). You can map the data into a very large feature space \(\varphi(x)\) without computing \(\varphi\), as long as you can compute the inner products. The matrix \(K\) is the Gram matrix.

Quantum kernels

Let the feature map be a circuit that prepares \(|\varphi(x)\rangle\). The kernel is

\[ \kappa(x,x')=|\langle\varphi(x')|\varphi(x)\rangle|^2, \]

which the swap test or the inversion test (III.4) estimates. The quantum computer fills in the Gram matrix; a classical SVM solver does the rest. Any advantage depends on choosing a feature map that is hard to simulate classically and still useful for the data.

IV.3Lecture 10/16

A distance-based quantum classifier

A complete classifier from Schuld, Fingerhuth & Petruccione (EPL 119, 60002, 2017; arXiv:1703.10793). After state preparation, the whole algorithm is one Hadamard gate and two measurements.

Key points
  • Four ways to combine data and processing: CC, CQ, QC, QQ. This is CQ: classical data on a quantum computer.
  • Entangle the test point with every training point, interfere them with one Hadamard, and postselect.
  • Each class gets probability proportional to \(\sum_m\big(1-\tfrac14|\tilde x-x_m|^2\big)\) over its training points.
  • The squared distance is used because it is what quantum interference produces naturally.

Where QML sits

CCClassical data, classical deviceOrdinary machine learning, or quantum-inspired classical algorithms.
CQClassical data, quantum deviceThis classifier, quantum kernels, and variational models.
QCQuantum data, classical deviceMachine learning used to analyze quantum experiments.
QQQuantum data, quantum deviceLearning directly from quantum states.

The state

Take \(M\) training vectors \(x_m\) with labels \(y_m\), and a new input \(\tilde x\). All are normalized and amplitude-encoded. Prepare four registers (index, ancilla, data, label):

\[ |D\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{2M}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}|m\rangle\Big(|0\rangle|\psi_{\tilde x}\rangle+|1\rangle|\psi_{x_m}\rangle\Big)|y_m\rangle . \]

The ancilla entangles the test point (branch 0) with each training point (branch 1).

The algorithm

  1. Apply H to the ancilla. The branches interfere, and the data register holds \(\tilde x+x_m\) when the ancilla is 0 and \(\tilde x-x_m\) when it is 1.
  2. Measure the ancilla and keep only the runs with result 0 (postselection). This succeeds with probability \(p_{\text{acc}}=\tfrac1{4M}\sum_m|\tilde x+x_m|^2\).
  3. Measure the label qubit. The probability of class \(c\) is
\[ P(y=c)=\frac{1}{4M\,p_{\text{acc}}}\sum_{m:\,y_m=c}|\tilde x+x_m|^2 . \]

For unit vectors \(|\tilde x+x|^2=4-|\tilde x-x|^2\), so each training point votes with weight \(1-\tfrac14|\tilde x-x_m|^2\). Close points vote strongly; distant points vote weakly. It is a nearest-neighbor rule with a squared-distance weight.

Toy examples from the lecture

Iris and Titanic

Iris (Fisher, 1936): two classes, two features (for example sepal length and width), one training point per class, and one test point. After standardizing and normalizing, each point is one angle on the unit circle, and the circuit fits in four qubits: index, ancilla, data, label.

Titanic (from Kaggle): two 2D training vectors with labels "survived" and "did not survive". The test passenger gets the label of the closer training passenger, with a probability set by the squared distances.

Five lessons from this example

  1. Data encoding is very important in quantum machine learning, especially for classical data. It changes the runtime and the principle of the algorithm.
  2. The quantum algorithm imposes preprocessing on the classical data, for example standardization and normalization.
  3. The result comes from measurements, so the experiment must be repeated many times.
  4. QML algorithms are often inspired by classical ones.
  5. Quantum hardware may require changes to the classical model. Here the squared distance was used because it fits the quantum formalism.
About the speed. The circuit after state preparation is constant size. The cost is in preparing \(|D\rangle\), which for arbitrary data takes time that grows with \(M\) and the dimension, and in the repetitions needed for good statistics. Do not read the method as an exponential speedup.

V.1

Complexity & quantum advantage

"Quantum is faster" is too vague. The real question is how a problem's cost grows with its size. For a specific set of problems, the quantum growth rate is much smaller.

Key points
  • Polynomial growth is efficient; exponential growth is not.
  • Speedups are problem-specific: exponential for Simon, Shor, and the QFT; quadratic for Grover.
  • Loading data and reading results often cancel the advantage. Check both ends.

Efficient and inefficient

An algorithm is efficient if its runtime grows polynomially with the input size \(n\), and inefficient if it grows exponentially. Going from \(n=1000\) to \(n=1050\), an \(n^4\) algorithm slows by about \((1050/1000)^4\approx1.2\). A \(2^n\) algorithm slows by \(2^{50}\), which turns one second into about 36 million years.

V.2Lecture 10/7

Quantum sensing & networks

The same qubits that compute also measure. A superconducting qubit is an extremely sensitive detector of weak microwave fields, such as those a dark-matter particle might produce.

Key points
  • A weak field drives \(|0\rangle\to|1\rangle\) with probability \(p_1\approx\varepsilon^2t^2\).
  • \(N\) independent sensors improve the rate by \(N\); \(N\) sensors in a GHZ state improve it by \(N^2\).
  • Large machines will be modular: smaller chips joined by photonic entanglement.

A qubit as a detector

A transmon (II.6) has a frequency of 1–10 GHz, a wavelength of 3–30 cm. At 10 mK, \(k_BT\ll\hbar\omega\), so the qubit sits in \(|0\rangle\). A weak oscillating field at the qubit frequency (for example, from a hypothetical dark-matter field) drives it slowly toward \(|1\rangle\):

\[ p_1\approx\varepsilon^2t^2 , \]

where \(\varepsilon\) is the coupling strength and \(t\) the interaction time. The time is limited by coherence, \(\tau\sim100\ \mu\text{s}\). So one experiment is many short cycles: about \(10^5\) cycles in 10 seconds for each frequency. Tuning the frequency scans dark-matter masses of about 4–40 μeV.

Many sensors, and entangled sensors

With \(N\) independent qubits, the chance that at least one flips grows linearly: \(p\approx N\varepsilon^2\tau^2\). Prepare the \(N\) qubits in a GHZ state instead and the phases add coherently, so \(p\approx N^2\varepsilon^2\tau^2\) (Chen et al., Phys. Rev. Lett., 2024). The lectures also cite work on distributed quantum sensing, where the entangled sensors are in different places (Fukuda et al.).

Toward quantum networks

One very large chip has practical limits: wiring for every qubit (input/output fan-out), crosstalk between neighbors, calibration effort, and noise. The planned alternative is modular: many smaller chips linked by entanglement carried by photons. The same links make sensor networks and quantum communication possible.

Glossary

Every recurring term on this page. Type in the box to filter the list.

Qubit
The basic unit of quantum information: a unit vector in a 2-dimensional complex space. Generalizes the classical bit, which can only be 0 or 1.
State vector |ψ⟩
The complete description of a qubit's state as a normalized column of complex amplitudes.
Amplitude α, β
The complex coefficients multiplying each basis state. Their squared magnitudes give measurement probabilities (the Born rule).
Computational basis |0⟩, |1⟩
The standard reference basis for a qubit. Measurements return one of these labels by default.
Normalization
The requirement that |α|² + |β|² = 1, so that measurement probabilities sum to one. Achieved by dividing a vector by its norm.
Born rule
The law that the probability of a measurement outcome equals the squared magnitude of that outcome's amplitude.
Dirac / bra-ket notation
|ψ⟩ ("ket") is a column vector; ⟨ψ| ("bra") is its conjugate transpose (a row vector). Together they form inner products ⟨φ|ψ⟩.
Inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩
A complex number measuring the overlap of two states. Computed as the conjugate of the first vector dotted with the second.
Orthonormal basis
A set of states that are each normalized (⟨v|v⟩ = 1) and mutually orthogonal (⟨v|w⟩ = 0). |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the canonical example.
Superposition
A state that is a nontrivial combination of basis states, e.g. |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. The source of quantum parallelism.
|+⟩ and |−⟩
The equal superpositions (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2. They are the eigenstates of Pauli X and form the "X basis."
Unitary matrix U
A matrix with U†U = I. Every valid, reversible quantum operation is a unitary; it preserves normalization.
Gate
A named, reusable unitary applied to specific wires — the building block of a circuit.
Global phase
An overall factor e^{iγ} multiplying the entire state. Physically undetectable — states differing only by it are identical.
Relative phase
A phase between basis components (e.g. |0⟩ + e^{iφ}|1⟩). Invisible in the computational basis but observable in others; the engine of interference.
Hadamard H
The gate (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]. Sends |0⟩→|+⟩ and |1⟩→|−⟩, creating uniform superposition. Its own inverse.
Pauli X X
The bit-flip gate: |0⟩↔|1⟩. The quantum NOT gate.
Pauli Y Y
The gate [[0,−i],[i,0]]. Combines a bit-flip and a phase-flip; eigenstates are |y±⟩ = (|0⟩ ± i|1⟩)/√2.
Pauli Z Z
The phase-flip gate: |0⟩→|0⟩, |1⟩→−|1⟩. Leaves computational-basis probabilities unchanged.
Eigenvalue / eigenstate
For an observable, the possible measurement results (eigenvalues, ±1 for a Pauli) and the states that return them with certainty (eigenstates).
Rotation gates RX, RY, RZ
Continuous single-axis rotations of the Bloch sphere by an angle θ. Note the half-angle: RX(θ) uses cos(θ/2), sin(θ/2).
Rot gate
The general single-qubit unitary Rot(φ,θ,ω) = RZ(ω)·RY(θ)·RZ(φ), parametrized by three Euler angles.
S gate
The phase gate: adds a relative phase of i to |1⟩. Equals RZ(π/2) up to a global phase; S = √Z.
T gate
Adds a phase e^{iπ/4} to |1⟩. Equals RZ(π/4) up to a global phase; T = √S. With H it forms a universal gate set.
Adjoint / dagger U†
The inverse of a unitary (its conjugate transpose). In code, qp.adjoint(gate) undoes an operation; RZ†(ω) = RZ(−ω).
Universal gate set
A small set of gates that can build (or approximate to any precision) every unitary. Examples: {RZ, RY}, {RZ, RX}, {H, T}.
Euler (ZXZ / ZYZ) decomposition
The fact that any single-qubit unitary equals three rotations about alternating axes — the reason three parameters suffice.
Circuit synthesis
Building a target unitary from a fixed gate set. Part of the broader field of quantum compilation.
QNode
A PennyLane object binding a quantum function to a device, so the circuit can be called like an ordinary Python function.
Device
The backend that runs a circuit — a simulator (default.qubit) or real hardware. Created with a wire count and optional shot count.
Wire
PennyLane's name for a qubit / register line in a circuit, identified by an integer or label.
Circuit depth
The number of sequential gate layers — the longest path through the circuit's dependency graph. A rough proxy for runtime and error accumulation.
Measurement
Extracting classical information from a state, collapsing it to an eigenstate of the measured observable with Born-rule probability.
Expectation value ⟨A⟩
The probability-weighted average of an observable's eigenvalues in a state: ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩. For a Pauli, it lies in [−1, 1].
Observable
A Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes (e.g. PauliZ, or a tensor product Z⊗X).
Shots
The number of times a circuit is run and measured to estimate a quantity. Analytic (exact) results correspond to infinite shots.
Sampling
Drawing individual measurement outcomes rather than computing an average — what real hardware does.
Variance & the 1/N law
The spread of a shot-based estimate. Averaging N shots divides the single-shot variance by N, so error shrinks as 1/√N.
Basis change measurement
To measure in a non-computational basis, apply the adjoint of the rotation that creates that basis, then measure normally.
Tensor product ⊗
How independent subsystems combine. Two qubits live in a 4-dimensional space; observables combine with @ in code.
Basis state |n⟩
A multi-qubit computational-basis state labeled by a bitstring; qp.BasisState prepares one from a list of bits.
Separable / product state
A multi-qubit state that factorizes, e.g. |+⟩⊗|1⟩. Its measurements on different qubits are independent.
Factorization of ⟨A⊗B⟩
For a product state, the joint expectation splits: ⟨A⊗B⟩ = ⟨A⟩⟨B⟩. This fails once qubits are entangled.
Template
A pre-built subroutine (e.g. StatePrep) usable in a circuit like a single gate. Decomposes into basic gates on the device.
StatePrep / Möttönen's method
A template that prepares any normalized state vector, expanding into a sequence of RY/RZ rotations (Möttönen decomposition).
Bloch sphere
The geometric picture of a single-qubit state as a point on a unit sphere; gates are rotations of it.
Hilbert space
The complex vector space (with an inner product) where quantum states live. One qubit uses ℂ²; n qubits use ℂ^(2ⁿ).
Wavefunction
The state described as a function of amplitudes. For a qubit it is just the state vector; the term is standard in continuous systems.
Complex amplitude
A state coefficient carrying both magnitude and phase. The phase enables interference; only the squared magnitude is observable.
Interference
Amplitudes for the same outcome adding or canceling. Algorithms engineer wrong answers to cancel and right ones to reinforce.
Postulates of QM
The four rules: state = unit vector, evolution = unitary, measurement = probabilistic collapse (Born rule), composite = tensor product.
Schrödinger equation
iħ d|ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|ψ⟩ — the continuous law of unitary evolution driven by the Hamiltonian.
Hamiltonian Ĥ
The energy operator of a system. Its structure determines how a state evolves in time.
Hermitian operator
A matrix equal to its conjugate transpose (A = A†). Represents an observable: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.
Collapse
The abrupt, irreversible change of a state to a measured eigenstate. The one non-unitary step in the theory.
Uncertainty / commuting
Two observables can be known together only if AB = BA. X, Y, Z do not commute, so their values are mutually uncertain (Heisenberg).
Pauli matrices σx σy σz
The three fundamental single-qubit observables/gates, X, Y, Z. Also the generators of the rotation gates.
Bell state
A maximally entangled two-qubit state, e.g. (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. The standard example of correlations with no classical analog.
Decoherence
Loss of quantum behavior as a qubit couples to its environment, scrambling relative phases. The main source of hardware error and noise.
No-cloning theorem
An unknown quantum state cannot be copied. Rules out backing up a state to avoid destructive measurement.
Planck's constant h, ħ
The fundamental quantum of action (h ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s). Sets the scale where physics becomes quantized.
Quantum supremacy / advantage
A quantum device solving a task faster than any classical computer. Problem-specific, not a blanket speedup.
Completeness relation
Σ|i⟩⟨i| = I for any orthonormal basis. Lets you "insert a basis" anywhere and read off matrix elements.
Outer product |a⟩⟨b|
A ket times a bra — a matrix (operator), as opposed to the inner product, which is a number.
Generator
The Hermitian G in U = e^{iθG}. Every unitary is an exponentiated observable; the Paulis generate single-qubit rotations.
Unitary group U(N) / SU(N)
The group of N×N unitaries; SU(N) adds det = 1. Single-qubit gates form SU(2), hence three parameters.
Commutator [A,B]
AB − BA. Zero when operators can be measured together; the anticommutator is AB + BA.
Adiabatic theorem
A system in an eigenstate stays there if the Hamiltonian changes slowly enough. Basis of adiabatic computing and annealing.
Spin-½
The cleanest physical qubit: |0⟩ = spin up, |1⟩ = spin down. Source of the term "qubit as a spin."
Transmon
A superconducting qubit whose anharmonic energy levels isolate a clean two-level |0⟩/|1⟩ subspace. Today's dominant platform.
Transpilation
Rewriting an abstract circuit to a device's native gates and qubit connectivity — usually lengthening it.
Coupling map / topology
Which qubit pairs on a chip can directly interact. Determines how many SWAPs a circuit needs.
Fidelity / error rate
How accurately a gate or measurement performs. Below 1 on all real hardware.
NISQ
Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum: today's regime of tens–hundreds of noisy, uncorrected qubits.
Kronecker product
The component-level form of the tensor product: stacks scaled copies of one vector/matrix by the entries of another.
Direct sum ⊕
Combining spaces by stacking bases and adding dimensions (operators act block-diagonally). Contrast the tensor product, which multiplies.
Partial trace
Tracing out one subsystem to get the effective (reduced) state of the rest of an entangled system.
CNOT / controlled-U
Two-qubit gates that act on a target only when the control is |1⟩. CNOT flips; controlled-U applies any single-qubit gate.
CZ / CPhase(φ)
Puts a phase on |11⟩ only. CPhase(π) = CZ, a −1 on |11⟩; symmetric in its two qubits.
SWAP gate
Exchanges two qubits, |ab⟩ → |ba⟩. Equals ½(I⊗I + X⊗X + Y⊗Y + Z⊗Z).
GHZ state
The three-qubit (or N-qubit) maximally entangled state (|000⟩+|111⟩)/√2.
Superdense coding
Sending two classical bits by transmitting one qubit, using a pre-shared Bell pair.
Quantum teleportation
Moving an unknown state to a distant qubit using a shared Bell pair and two classical bits. The original is destroyed.
Oracle U_f
A reversible black-box unitary encoding a function: |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y⊕f(x)⟩.
Reversible computation
Logic that can run backwards. Required because quantum gates are unitary; Toffoli and Fredkin are universal reversible gates.
Toffoli / Fredkin
Three-qubit gates (controlled-controlled-NOT and controlled-SWAP) that make classical logic reversible.
Quantum parallelism
Evaluating a function on a superposition of all inputs at once — though measurement returns only one result.
Phase kickback
With the target in |−⟩, an oracle stamps (−1)^{f(x)} onto the input's phase instead of flipping the target. Powers Deutsch-type algorithms.
Query complexity
The number of oracle calls an algorithm needs. The setting where quantum advantage is first proven.
Feature map / data encoding
The circuit that loads classical data into a quantum state. Schemes: basis, amplitude, angle, time-evolution, Hamiltonian.
Data re-uploading
Feeding the same input into a circuit repeatedly between trainable gates, making even one qubit a universal classifier.
Variational quantum algorithm (VQA / VQE)
A parametrized circuit trained by a classical optimizer to minimize a measured cost. VQE targets ground-state energies.
Quantum annealing
Solving optimization by slowly evolving to the ground state of a problem Hamiltonian (the adiabatic theorem in practice).
QAOA
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm — a near-term variational method for combinatorial problems like max-cut.
Polynomial vs exponential
Efficient algorithms scale as a power of the input size; inefficient ones scale as 2ⁿ. The line between easy and hard.
Quantum error correction
Protecting information by spreading it over many physical qubits (bit-flip/phase-flip codes, stabilizers). The threshold theorem: below a threshold error rate, more qubits mean a more reliable logical qubit.
Hamming weight / distance
The number of 1-bits in a string (weight), or the number of positions where two strings differ (distance). Appears in the bit-string identities behind Deutsch–Jozsa.
Entanglement
A multi-qubit state that does not factorize, such as a Bell state. Measuring one qubit then tells you about the others.
Walsh–Hadamard transform
W = H^⊗n. W|x⟩ = 2^{−n/2} Σ_y (−1)^{x·y}|y⟩; W|0…0⟩ is the uniform superposition. Its own inverse.
Deutsch / Deutsch–Jozsa
Decide whether a function is constant or balanced with one oracle query. Deterministic classical algorithms can need 2^{n−1}+1.
Bernstein–Vazirani
Recover a hidden string s from f(x) = s·x mod 2 in one query, versus n classically.
Simon's algorithm
Find a hidden XOR period a with f(x) = f(x⊕a). Each run gives y with y·a = 0; O(n) queries versus ~2^{n/2} classically.
Swap test
H, controlled-SWAP, H on an ancilla: P(0) = ½ + ½|⟨a|b⟩|². Estimates the overlap of two states.
Shor's algorithm
Factors N in polynomial time by finding the order of a random a mod N with phase estimation. Breaks RSA.
Hidden subgroup problem
Find a subgroup H when f is constant on its cosets. Simon, factoring, and discrete log are abelian cases with efficient quantum algorithms.
Grover's algorithm
Find a marked item among N with about (π/4)√N oracle calls: a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.
Ancilla
An extra helper qubit, such as the |−⟩ target of an oracle or the control qubit of a swap test.
Constant / balanced function
Constant: the same output for every input. Balanced: output 0 on exactly half the inputs. The promise in Deutsch–Jozsa.
Bitwise dot product x·y
Σ xᵢyᵢ, the number of positions where both strings are 1. Taken mod 2 in signs (−1)^{x·y}.
Orthogonality sum
Σₓ(−1)^{x·y} = 2ⁿ if y = 0 and 0 otherwise. The identity behind Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani.
Birthday bound
Random guessing finds a collision f(x) = f(y) after about √(number of outputs) tries. Why classical Simon needs ~2^{n/2} queries.
Postselection
Keeping only the runs where a measurement gave a chosen result, and discarding the rest.
Hadamard test
An ancilla circuit with P(0) = ½(1 + Re⟨a|b⟩). A phase gate on the ancilla gives the imaginary part.
Inversion test
Apply U_a, then U_b†, and measure: P(0…0) = |⟨b|a⟩|². No ancilla needed.
Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
f̃ⱼ = (1/√N) Σₖ w^{−jk} fₖ with w = e^{2πi/N}. Changes samples into frequencies. N² operations directly.
Fast Fourier transform (FFT)
The Cooley–Tukey algorithm: split into even and odd terms recursively to compute the DFT in O(N log N).
Root of unity
A complex number w with wᴺ = 1. The primitive one is e^{2πi/N}.
Convolution theorem
The Fourier transform of a convolution is the pointwise product of the transforms. Makes fast polynomial multiplication possible.
Quantum Fourier transform (QFT)
F|j⟩ = 2^{−n/2} Σₖ e^{2πijk/2ⁿ}|k⟩. O(n²) gates, but the output is a state, not a list of numbers.
Binary fraction 0.j₁j₂…
j₁/2 + j₂/4 + …. The phases in the QFT product form and the output of phase estimation.
Controlled phase rotation R_k
diag(1, e^{2πi/2^k}), applied when a control qubit is 1. R₁ = Z, R₂ = S, R₃ = T. The QFT's building block.
Quantum phase estimation (QPE)
Finds θ in U|u⟩ = e^{2πiθ}|u⟩ with counting qubits, controlled-U^{2^j} gates, and the inverse QFT.
Eigenphase
The angle θ of a unitary's eigenvalue e^{2πiθ}. All eigenvalues of a unitary have size 1.
Modular arithmetic / congruence
Arithmetic on remainders. a ≡ b (mod N) means N divides a − b.
Order r
The smallest r > 0 with aʳ ≡ 1 (mod N). The period of x ↦ aˣ mod N. Finding it is the quantum step of Shor.
Repeated squaring
Computing aˣ mod N by squaring a and multiplying the powers for the 1 bits of x. O(log x) multiplications.
Modular inverse
a⁻¹ with a·a⁻¹ ≡ 1 (mod N). Exists exactly when gcd(a, N) = 1. Found by the extended Euclidean algorithm.
Euclid's algorithm
gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b), repeated until the remainder is 0. Fast: O(log min(a, b)) steps.
Fermat's little theorem / Euler's theorem
a^{p−1} ≡ 1 (mod p) for prime p. More generally a^{φ(N)} ≡ 1 (mod N) when gcd(a, N) = 1.
Euler's totient φ(N)
How many numbers below N are coprime to N. For N = pq, φ = (p−1)(q−1).
RSA
Public-key encryption: b = a^c mod N, a = b^d mod N, with cd ≡ 1 (mod φ(N)). Secure while factoring N is hard.
Order finding
Phase estimation on U|y⟩ = |ay mod N⟩. Its eigenphases are s/r, so it reveals the order r.
Continued fractions
The classical method that turns the measured c/Q into a fraction k/r with a small denominator, giving r.
Number field sieve (GNFS)
The best known classical factoring algorithm, with sub-exponential cost exp(1.9 (log N)^{1/3} (log log N)^{2/3}).
Discrete logarithm
Given b, y, and p, find x with bˣ ≡ y (mod p). The basis of Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography; Shor's method solves it.
Group / abelian group
A set with an associative operation, an identity, and inverses. Abelian if the operation commutes.
Coset
A shifted copy g + H of a subgroup H. A hidden subgroup function is constant on each coset.
Post-quantum cryptography
Classical encryption built on problems with no known fast quantum algorithm, such as lattice problems.
Diffusion operator S
2|ψ₀⟩⟨ψ₀| − I: a reflection about the uniform superposition. Built from H^⊗n, a multi-controlled gate, and H^⊗n.
Grover iteration G = SO
Oracle then diffusion. Two reflections make a rotation by 2θ₀ toward the marked state.
Amplitude amplification
The general form of Grover: repeat reflections to grow the amplitude of a good subspace. Used as a subroutine in many algorithms.
Quantum counting
Phase estimation on the Grover iteration, whose eigenvalues e^{±2iθ₀} reveal the number of marked items.
Multi-controlled X
A NOT on the target when every control has its chosen value. qp.MultiControlledX with control_values builds Grover oracles.
QRAM
Quantum random-access memory: a device that would load classical data into superposition quickly. Many speedups assume it; it does not exist at scale.
Support vector machine (SVM)
A linear classifier sign(w·x + b) trained to maximize the margin between classes.
Margin / support vectors
The margin is the distance from the decision boundary to the closest points. Those closest points are the support vectors.
Soft margin / hinge loss
Allows some errors with slack ξᵢ and penalty C. Equivalent to the hinge loss max(0, 1 − yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b)).
Kernel trick / Gram matrix
Replace inner products with k(x, x′) = φ(x)·φ(x′), computed without forming φ. The matrix Kᵢⱼ = k(xᵢ, xⱼ) is the Gram matrix.
Quantum kernel
κ(x, x′) = |⟨φ(x′)|φ(x)⟩|² for a circuit feature map, estimated with an overlap test.
Distance-based classifier
Schuld et al. (2017): one Hadamard and postselection give class probabilities weighted by 1 − ¼|x̃ − xₘ|².
CC / CQ / QC / QQ
The four combinations of classical or quantum data with a classical or quantum device.
Hyperspherical encoding
Writing an n-dimensional unit vector as n − 1 angles, which rotations can load.
Standardization / normalization
Shift and scale data to zero mean and unit variance; then scale each vector to unit length so it can be a quantum state.
Josephson junction
A thin insulating barrier between superconductors. Its nonlinearity makes the transmon's energy levels uneven.
Quantum sensing
Using qubits as detectors. N entangled (GHZ) sensors can reach a signal rate that grows as N² instead of N.
Modular quantum computer
Many small chips joined by photonic entanglement, instead of one very large chip.
