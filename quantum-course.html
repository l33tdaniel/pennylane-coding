Foundations & how to read this

Everything downstream is built from four objects: a state, an operation, a measurement, and the probabilities that connect them. Fix those and the rest of the course is bookkeeping.

The single qubit

A qubit lives in a two-dimensional complex vector space. Its state is a unit-length column vector written in Dirac notation as \[ |\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|0\rangle + \beta\,|1\rangle,\qquad |0\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ |1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}. \] Here \(\psi\) is the Greek letter psi (the symbol for a state), and \(\alpha,\beta\) are alpha and beta. The complex numbers \(\alpha,\beta\) are amplitudes. They are not probabilities — their squared magnitudes are. Measuring in the computational basis returns 0 with probability \(|\alpha|^2\) and 1 with probability \(|\beta|^2\), which is why a physical state must satisfy the normalization condition \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\).

Three things you can do to it

Evolve it with a unitary matrix \(U\): the new state is \(|\psi'\rangle = U|\psi\rangle\). Measure it, collapsing the superposition to a basis outcome drawn from the Born-rule probabilities. Or ask for an expectation value — the average measurement result of an observable, which a simulator can compute exactly and hardware can only estimate by sampling.

Phase is the whole game

Two states that differ only by an overall factor \(e^{i\gamma}\) — a global phase, where \(\gamma\) is the Greek letter gamma — are physically identical; no measurement can tell them apart. A relative phase between \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\) is different: it is invisible to a computational-basis measurement but changes the outcome in other bases, and it is the resource interference exploits. Half of this course is learning to place, move, and read phases.

Reading the notation

Circuits are drawn left-to-right; each wire is a qubit, gates are boxes, and [M] marks a measurement. In code the library is imported as qp (PennyLane's conventional alias is qml; this course aliases it qp). Code blocks show the essential lines, not the boilerplate.

Convention that bites everyone: PennyLane numbers qubits left-to-right, so \(|10100\rangle\) puts wires 0 and 2 in \(|1\rangle\). Many textbooks and other frameworks make qubit 0 the rightmost bit. Always confirm the ordering with a new library.

The physics underneath

The code treats a qubit as a vector and a gate as a matrix, but that vector describes something real. This section is the quantum mechanics the exercises assume — where the rules come from, what a measurement physically does, and why any of this buys you computing power.

Where quantum mechanics comes from

Classical physics failed to explain a handful of experiments around 1900: hot objects didn't glow the way theory predicted, and light knocked electrons out of metals in discrete lumps. The fix, from Planck and Einstein, was that energy comes in indivisible packets — quanta. Energy is \(E=h\nu\), where \(\nu\) (Greek nu) is the frequency and \(h\) is Planck's constant, a tiny fixed number (\(6.626\times10^{-34}\) J·s) that sets the scale at which the world stops being smooth and starts being grainy. Everything quantum is a consequence of that graininess.

The wavefunction and why the numbers are complex

A quantum system is described by a state vector (in continuous systems, a wavefunction) that lives in an abstract space called a Hilbert space — just a complex vector space with an inner product. The amplitudes are complex numbers for a physical reason: a complex number carries both a size and an angle (a phase), and it is the interplay of phases that produces interference. Real probabilities alone could never do that. When two paths to the same outcome have amplitudes that point in opposite directions, they cancel; when they align, they reinforce. That is the double-slit experiment, and it is the whole reason quantum computers can outrun classical ones on certain problems.

Superposition — what it does and doesn't mean

A qubit in \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) is in a superposition. The honest statement is not "it is both 0 and 1 at once" — it is that the system has a definite amplitude for each, and only the act of measuring forces a single outcome. Before measurement the amplitudes evolve together and can interfere; a quantum algorithm is choreography that arranges for the wrong answers' amplitudes to cancel and the right answer's to survive.

The four postulates of quantum mechanics

The entire theory rests on four rules. Every exercise in this course is one of them in action.

  • Postulate 1 · StateA system is a unit vector in a Hilbert space. For one qubit that is \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) with \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\). This is Module I.1.
  • Postulate 2 · EvolutionClosed-system evolution is unitary. States change by \(|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle\) for a unitary \(U\). Continuously, this is the Schrödinger equation \(i\hbar\,\tfrac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = \hat H|\psi\rangle\), where \(\hat H\) (the Hamiltonian) is the energy operator and \(\hbar\) is Planck's constant over \(2\pi\). Gates are just unitaries. This is I.3–I.8.
  • Postulate 3 · MeasurementMeasurement is probabilistic and irreversible. Measuring an observable yields one of its eigenvalues; the probability is the squared amplitude of the state along that eigenvector (the Born rule), and the state collapses to that eigenvector. This is I.9–I.10.
  • Postulate 4 · Composite systemsSystems combine by tensor product. Two qubits live in a 4-dimensional space \(\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2\). This is where entanglement becomes possible. This is I.11.

Observables, Hermitian operators, and eigenvalues

A measurable quantity — spin along an axis, energy, the value of a Pauli — is represented by a Hermitian operator (a matrix equal to its own conjugate transpose, \(A=A^\dagger\)). Two facts make this the right object: a Hermitian operator's eigenvalues are always real (so they can be actual meter readings), and its eigenvectors are orthogonal (so the possible outcomes are distinguishable). Measuring \(A\) can only return one of its eigenvalues; the Pauli operators have eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\), which is why every Pauli expectation value lands in \([-1,1]\). The expectation value \(\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle\) is the average reading over many identical runs.

Incompatible observables and uncertainty

You cannot read every property at once. Two observables can be measured simultaneously only if they commute — if \(AB=BA\). The Pauli operators do not commute with one another, so a state with a definite \(Z\) value (like \(|0\rangle\)) has a completely uncertain \(X\) value, and vice versa. This is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle in its cleanest form, and it is the practical reason you must choose a basis to measure in — and rotate into it, as in I.9.

The Bloch sphere — a picture of one qubit

Any single-qubit state (ignoring global phase) can be written with two angles: \(|\psi\rangle=\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle\), where \(\theta\) (theta) is a polar angle and \(\varphi\) (phi) an azimuthal angle. Those are spherical coordinates, so every state is a point on a sphere — the Bloch sphere. The north pole is \(|0\rangle\), the south pole \(|1\rangle\), and the equator holds the superpositions \(|+\rangle,|-\rangle,|y_\pm\rangle\). Then the rotation gates are literally rotations: \(RX,RY,RZ\) spin the sphere about the x-, y-, and z-axes. This is why the angle appears as \(\theta/2\): the state's amplitudes turn at half the Bloch-vector angle (the SU(2) double cover of the sphere's rotations), so a full \(2\pi\) rotation brings the Bloch vector back to start while multiplying the state by \(-1\).

Global phase as unobservable freedom

Because the Born rule squares amplitudes, an overall \(e^{i\gamma}\) drops out of every probability. The global phase is gauge freedom — real mathematics, no physical consequence — which is why the course so often says "up to a global phase." A relative phase is different: it survives into measurement probabilities the moment you change basis.

Entanglement and nonlocality

Postulate 4 allows states of two qubits that cannot be written as a product of single-qubit states — entangled states like the Bell state \((|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Measuring one qubit instantly determines the other, no matter how far apart. That single-basis correlation on its own could still be faked by classical shared randomness; what has no classical explanation is the full pattern of correlations across incompatible measurement settings, which exceeds any local hidden-variable model — the real content of Bell's theorem. The states in Module I.11 are deliberately separable, so their expectation values factorize — but entanglement, created by two-qubit gates like CNOT, is the resource that gives quantum computing its edge.

Decoherence, noise, and why hardware samples

An isolated qubit evolves unitarily forever; a real one is coupled to its environment, which continuously "measures" it and scrambles the delicate relative phases. This leakage is decoherence, and it is why real devices are noisy and why I.10 works with finite shots and statistical error instead of exact values. The no-cloning theorem — you cannot copy an unknown quantum state — means you also can't dodge the problem by making backups. Managing decoherence is the central engineering challenge of the field.

What a qubit physically is

The math is substrate-independent; the hardware is not. A qubit can be the spin of an electron or nucleus (up/down), the polarization of a photon (horizontal/vertical), two energy levels of a trapped ion, or — most common in today's processors — the two lowest states of a superconducting circuit (a transmon). In every case the two chosen levels are relabelled \(|0\rangle\) and \(|1\rangle\), and the abstract gates of this course are realized as carefully shaped microwave or laser pulses.

Key-term glossary

Every recurring term in the course, in one place. Search to jump to a definition; each idea is developed further in the module it first appears.

Qubit
The basic unit of quantum information: a unit vector in a 2-dimensional complex space. Generalizes the classical bit, which can only be 0 or 1.
State vector |ψ⟩
The complete description of a qubit's state as a normalized column of complex amplitudes.
Amplitude α, β
The complex coefficients multiplying each basis state. Their squared magnitudes give measurement probabilities (the Born rule).
Computational basis |0⟩, |1⟩
The standard reference basis for a qubit. Measurements return one of these labels by default.
Normalization
The requirement that |α|² + |β|² = 1, so that measurement probabilities sum to one. Achieved by dividing a vector by its norm.
Born rule
The law that the probability of a measurement outcome equals the squared magnitude of that outcome's amplitude.
Dirac / bra-ket notation
|ψ⟩ ("ket") is a column vector; ⟨ψ| ("bra") is its conjugate transpose (a row vector). Together they form inner products ⟨φ|ψ⟩.
Inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩
A complex number measuring the overlap of two states. Computed as the conjugate of the first vector dotted with the second.
Orthonormal basis
A set of states that are each normalized (⟨v|v⟩ = 1) and mutually orthogonal (⟨v|w⟩ = 0). |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the canonical example.
Superposition
A state that is a nontrivial combination of basis states, e.g. |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. The source of quantum parallelism.
|+⟩ and |−⟩
The equal superpositions (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2. They are the eigenstates of Pauli X and form the "X basis."
Unitary matrix U
A matrix with U†U = I. Every valid, reversible quantum operation is a unitary; it preserves normalization.
Gate
A named, reusable unitary applied to specific wires — the building block of a circuit.
Global phase
An overall factor e^{iγ} multiplying the entire state. Physically undetectable — states differing only by it are identical.
Relative phase
A phase between basis components (e.g. |0⟩ + e^{iφ}|1⟩). Invisible in the computational basis but observable in others; the engine of interference.
Hadamard H
The gate (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]. Sends |0⟩→|+⟩ and |1⟩→|−⟩, creating uniform superposition. Its own inverse.
Pauli X X
The bit-flip gate: |0⟩↔|1⟩. The quantum NOT gate.
Pauli Y Y
The gate [[0,−i],[i,0]]. Combines a bit-flip and a phase-flip; eigenstates are |y±⟩ = (|0⟩ ± i|1⟩)/√2.
Pauli Z Z
The phase-flip gate: |0⟩→|0⟩, |1⟩→−|1⟩. Leaves computational-basis probabilities unchanged.
Eigenvalue / eigenstate
For an observable, the possible measurement results (eigenvalues, ±1 for a Pauli) and the states that return them with certainty (eigenstates).
Rotation gates RX, RY, RZ
Continuous single-axis rotations of the Bloch sphere by an angle θ. Note the half-angle: RX(θ) uses cos(θ/2), sin(θ/2).
Rot gate
The general single-qubit unitary Rot(φ,θ,ω) = RZ(ω)·RY(θ)·RZ(φ), parametrized by three Euler angles.
S gate
The phase gate: adds a relative phase of i to |1⟩. Equals RZ(π/2) up to a global phase; S = √Z.
T gate
Adds a phase e^{iπ/4} to |1⟩. Equals RZ(π/4) up to a global phase; T = √S. With H it forms a universal gate set.
Adjoint / dagger U†
The inverse of a unitary (its conjugate transpose). In code, qp.adjoint(gate) undoes an operation; RZ†(ω) = RZ(−ω).
Universal gate set
A small set of gates that can build (or approximate to any precision) every unitary. Examples: {RZ, RY}, {RZ, RX}, {H, T}.
Euler (ZXZ / ZYZ) decomposition
The fact that any single-qubit unitary equals three rotations about alternating axes — the reason three parameters suffice.
Circuit synthesis
Building a target unitary from a fixed gate set. Part of the broader field of quantum compilation.
QNode
A PennyLane object binding a quantum function to a device, so the circuit can be called like an ordinary Python function.
Device
The backend that runs a circuit — a simulator (default.qubit) or real hardware. Created with a wire count and optional shot count.
Wire
PennyLane's name for a qubit / register line in a circuit, identified by an integer or label.
Circuit depth
The number of sequential gate layers — the longest path through the circuit's dependency graph. A rough proxy for runtime and error accumulation.
Measurement
Extracting classical information from a state, collapsing it to an eigenstate of the measured observable with Born-rule probability.
Expectation value ⟨A⟩
The probability-weighted average of an observable's eigenvalues in a state: ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩. For a Pauli, it lies in [−1, 1].
Observable
A Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes (e.g. PauliZ, or a tensor product Z⊗X).
Shots
The number of times a circuit is run and measured to estimate a quantity. Analytic (exact) results correspond to infinite shots.
Sampling
Drawing individual measurement outcomes rather than computing an average — what real hardware does.
Variance & the 1/N law
The spread of a shot-based estimate. Averaging N shots divides the single-shot variance by N, so error shrinks as 1/√N.
Basis change measurement
To measure in a non-computational basis, apply the adjoint of the rotation that creates that basis, then measure normally.
Tensor product ⊗
How independent subsystems combine. Two qubits live in a 4-dimensional space; observables combine with @ in code.
Basis state |n⟩
A multi-qubit computational-basis state labelled by a bitstring; qp.BasisState prepares one from a list of bits.
Separable / product state
A multi-qubit state that factorizes, e.g. |+⟩⊗|1⟩. Its measurements on different qubits are independent.
Entanglement
A multi-qubit state that does not factorize; measuring one qubit affects the others. Absent here, but the reason two-qubit gates matter.
Factorization of ⟨A⊗B⟩
For a product state, the joint expectation splits: ⟨A⊗B⟩ = ⟨A⟩⟨B⟩. This fails once qubits are entangled.
Template
A pre-built subroutine (e.g. StatePrep) usable in a circuit like a single gate. Decomposes into basic gates on the device.
StatePrep / Möttönen's method
A template that prepares any normalized state vector, expanding into a sequence of RY/RZ rotations (Möttönen decomposition).
Bloch sphere
The geometric picture of a single-qubit state as a point on a unit sphere; gates are rotations of it.
Hilbert space
The complex vector space (with an inner product) where quantum states live. One qubit uses ℂ²; n qubits use ℂ^(2ⁿ).
Wavefunction
The state described as a function of amplitudes. For a qubit it is just the state vector; the term is standard in continuous systems.
Complex amplitude
A state coefficient carrying both magnitude and phase. The phase enables interference; only the squared magnitude is observable.
Interference
Amplitudes for the same outcome adding or cancelling. Algorithms engineer wrong answers to cancel and right ones to reinforce.
Postulates of QM
The four rules: state = unit vector, evolution = unitary, measurement = probabilistic collapse (Born rule), composite = tensor product.
Schrödinger equation
iħ d|ψ⟩/dt = Ĥ|ψ⟩ — the continuous law of unitary evolution driven by the Hamiltonian.
Hamiltonian Ĥ
The energy operator of a system. Its structure determines how a state evolves in time.
Hermitian operator
A matrix equal to its conjugate transpose (A = A†). Represents an observable: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.
Collapse
The abrupt, irreversible change of a state to a measured eigenstate. The one non-unitary step in the theory.
Uncertainty / commuting
Two observables can be known together only if AB = BA. X, Y, Z do not commute, so their values are mutually uncertain (Heisenberg).
Pauli matrices σx σy σz
The three fundamental single-qubit observables/gates, X, Y, Z. Also the generators of the rotation gates.
Bell state
A maximally entangled two-qubit state, e.g. (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. The standard example of correlations with no classical analog.
Decoherence
Loss of quantum behavior as a qubit couples to its environment, scrambling relative phases. The main source of hardware error and noise.
No-cloning theorem
An unknown quantum state cannot be copied. Rules out backing up a state to avoid destructive measurement.
Planck's constant h, ħ
The fundamental quantum of action (h ≈ 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s). Sets the scale where physics becomes quantized.
Quantum supremacy / advantage
A quantum device solving a task faster than any classical computer. Problem-specific, not a blanket speedup.
Completeness relation
Σ|i⟩⟨i| = I for any orthonormal basis. Lets you "insert a basis" anywhere and read off matrix elements.
Outer product |a⟩⟨b|
A ket times a bra — a matrix (operator), as opposed to the inner product, which is a number.
Generator
The Hermitian G in U = e^{iθG}. Every unitary is an exponentiated observable; the Paulis generate single-qubit rotations.
Unitary group U(N) / SU(N)
The group of N×N unitaries; SU(N) adds det = 1. Single-qubit gates form SU(2), hence three parameters.
Commutator [A,B]
AB − BA. Zero when operators can be measured together; the anticommutator is AB + BA.
Adiabatic theorem
A system in an eigenstate stays there if the Hamiltonian changes slowly enough. Basis of adiabatic computing and annealing.
Spin-½
The cleanest physical qubit: |0⟩ = spin up, |1⟩ = spin down. Source of the term "qubit as a spin."
Transmon
A superconducting qubit whose anharmonic energy levels isolate a clean two-level |0⟩/|1⟩ subspace. Today's dominant platform.
Transpilation
Rewriting an abstract circuit to a device's native gates and qubit connectivity — usually lengthening it.
Coupling map / topology
Which qubit pairs on a chip can directly interact. Determines how many SWAPs a circuit needs.
Fidelity / error rate
How accurately a gate or measurement performs. Below 1 on all real hardware.
NISQ
Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum: today's regime of tens–hundreds of noisy, uncorrected qubits.
Kronecker product
The component-level form of the tensor product: stacks scaled copies of one vector/matrix by the entries of another.
Direct sum ⊕
Combining spaces by stacking bases and adding dimensions (operators act block-diagonally). Contrast the tensor product, which multiplies.
Partial trace
Tracing out one subsystem to get the effective (reduced) state of the rest of an entangled system.
CNOT / controlled-U
Two-qubit gates that act on a target only when the control is |1⟩. CNOT flips; controlled-U applies any single-qubit gate.
CZ / CPhase(φ)
Puts a phase on |11⟩ only. CPhase(π) = CZ, a −1 on |11⟩; symmetric in its two qubits.
SWAP gate
Exchanges two qubits, |ab⟩ → |ba⟩. Equals ½(I⊗I + X⊗X + Y⊗Y + Z⊗Z).
GHZ state
The three-qubit (or N-qubit) maximally entangled state (|000⟩+|111⟩)/√2.
Superdense coding
Sending two classical bits by transmitting one qubit, using a pre-shared Bell pair.
Quantum teleportation
Moving an unknown state to a distant qubit using a shared Bell pair and two classical bits. The original is destroyed.
Oracle U_f
A reversible black-box unitary encoding a function: |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y⊕f(x)⟩.
Reversible computation
Logic that can run backwards. Required because quantum gates are unitary; Toffoli and Fredkin are universal reversible gates.
Toffoli / Fredkin
Three-qubit gates (controlled-controlled-NOT and controlled-SWAP) that make classical logic reversible.
Quantum parallelism
Evaluating a function on a superposition of all inputs at once — though measurement returns only one result.
Phase kickback
With the target in |−⟩, an oracle stamps (−1)^{f(x)} onto the input's phase instead of flipping the target. Powers Deutsch-type algorithms.
Walsh–Hadamard transform
W = H^⊗n: a Hadamard on every qubit, turning |0…0⟩ into an equal superposition of all 2ⁿ states.
Deutsch / Deutsch–Jozsa
Decide if a function is constant or balanced in one oracle query — the first proofs of quantum query speedup.
Bernstein–Vazirani
Recover a hidden bit-string s from f(x)=x·s in a single query, versus n queries classically.
Query complexity
The number of oracle calls an algorithm needs. The setting where quantum advantage is first proven.
Feature map / data encoding
The circuit that loads classical data into a quantum state. Schemes: basis, amplitude, angle, time-evolution, Hamiltonian.
Data re-uploading
Feeding the same input into a circuit repeatedly between trainable gates, making even one qubit a universal classifier.
Variational quantum algorithm (VQA / VQE)
A parametrized circuit trained by a classical optimizer to minimize a measured cost. VQE targets ground-state energies.
Swap test
An H – controlled-SWAP – H circuit on an ancilla that estimates the overlap |⟨a|b⟩|² of two states.
QFT & phase estimation
The quantum Fourier transform finds periodicity fast; phase estimation reads an eigenvalue's phase. Core of Shor's algorithm.
Shor's algorithm
Factors large integers in polynomial time, threatening RSA encryption. The field's most famous result.
Grover's algorithm
Finds a marked item among N in O(√N) steps — a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.
Quantum annealing
Solving optimization by slowly evolving to the ground state of a problem Hamiltonian (the adiabatic theorem in practice).
QAOA
Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm — a near-term variational method for combinatorial problems like max-cut.
Polynomial vs exponential
Efficient algorithms scale as a power of the input size; inefficient ones scale as 2ⁿ. The line between easy and hard.
Simon's algorithm
Finds a hidden period of a function with an exponential query speedup — the bridge from the oracle algorithms to Shor's period-finding core.
Quantum error correction
Protecting information by spreading it over many physical qubits (bit-flip/phase-flip codes, stabilizers). The threshold theorem: below a threshold error rate, more qubits mean a more reliable logical qubit.
Hidden subgroup problem
The abstract structure behind many quantum speedups (Simon, Shor). The discrete logarithm problem is another instance Shor's approach solves.
Hamming weight / distance
The number of 1-bits in a string (weight), or the number of positions where two strings differ (distance). Appears in the bit-string identities behind Deutsch–Jozsa.

I.1

All about qubits

Before any framework, build the qubit by hand in NumPy: a normalized vector, an inner product, a measurement drawn from probabilities, and a unitary applied as a matrix. These five exercises are the entire model in miniature.

I.1.1

Normalization of quantum states

An arbitrary combination \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) is not yet a physical state. Normalize it by dividing by its norm \(\sqrt{|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2}\), so the squared amplitudes — the measurement probabilities — sum to one.

solution
state = alpha * ket_0 + beta * ket_1
norm  = np.sqrt(np.abs(alpha)**2 + np.abs(beta)**2)
return state / norm
I.1.2

Inner product & orthonormal bases

The inner product \(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\) is the overlap of two states. The bra \(\langle\phi|\) is the conjugate transpose of the ket, so the computation is the conjugate of one vector dotted with the other. Verifying \(\langle0|0\rangle=\langle1|1\rangle=1\) and \(\langle0|1\rangle=0\) proves \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\) is orthonormal.

solution
# the bra ⟨s1| is the conjugate transpose of the ket |s1⟩
return np.dot(np.conj(state_1), state_2)
I.1.3

Sampling measurement outcomes

A measurement is a random draw. The probabilities are the squared amplitudes \(|\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2,\,|\langle1|\psi\rangle|^2\); simulate many measurements by sampling 0 or 1 from that distribution.

solution
probs = np.abs(state)**2
return np.random.choice([0, 1], size=num_meas, p=probs)
I.1.4

Applying a quantum operation

Operations are unitary matrices; the post-operation state is \(|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle\), a matrix–vector product. Here \(U\) is the Hadamard matrix — and note it is its own inverse, \(U(U|0\rangle)=|0\rangle\).

solution
U = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
return np.dot(U, state)
I.1.5

A simple quantum algorithm

Every quantum program is the same three steps: initialize a state, apply operations, measure. Chaining the three functions above reproduces the full pipeline in a single line — the template for everything that follows.

solution
return measure_state(apply_u(initialize_state()), 100)
I.2

Quantum circuits

PennyLane takes over the matrix bookkeeping. You describe a circuit as an ordinary Python function, bind it to a device as a QNode, and call it. Order matters, and so does depth.

I.2.1

Order of operations

Gates in a quantum function run top to bottom, so the line order must match the circuit's left-to-right order. Reordering scrambled gates to match the diagram is the whole task — a reminder that a circuit is a sequence, not a set.

circuit
|0> --*--[H]--(+)--------[M]
      |        |
|0> --(+)------|--[RY]---[M]
               |
|0> --[RX]-----*---------[M]
solution
qp.CNOT(wires=[0, 1])
qp.RX(theta, wires=2)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.CNOT(wires=[2, 0])
qp.RY(phi, wires=1)
return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])
I.2.2

Building a QNode

A QNode binds a quantum function to a device so it can be called like a normal function. Construct one explicitly with qp.QNode(func, dev). qp.probs returns the probability of every output basis state.

solution
dev = qp.device("default.qubit", wires=3)

def my_circuit(theta, phi, omega):
    qp.RX(theta, wires=0); qp.RY(phi, wires=1); qp.RZ(omega, wires=2)
    qp.CNOT(wires=[0,1]); qp.CNOT(wires=[1,2]); qp.CNOT(wires=[2,0])
    return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])

my_qnode = qp.QNode(my_circuit, dev)
I.2.3

The QNode decorator

The idiomatic shortcut: decorate the function with @qp.qnode(dev). It produces a QNode of the same name, already bound to the device — no separate construction step.

solution
@qp.qnode(dev)
def my_circuit(theta, phi, omega):
    ...
    return qp.probs(wires=[0, 1, 2])
I.2.4

Circuit depth

Depth is the number of layers that must run in sequence — the longest path through the circuit's dependency graph (DAG). Gates on different wires share a layer; a gate waits only for the wires it touches.

Layer 1: RX(θ), RY(φ), RZ(ω)   (parallel, different wires)
Layer 2: CNOT(0,1)
Layer 3: CNOT(1,2)             (waits for wire 1)
Layer 4: CNOT(2,0)            (waits for wire 2)   →  depth = 4
Verify, don't guess: qp.specs(my_circuit)(...)['resources'].depth returns the depth PennyLane computes, so you can check the count.
I.3

Unitary matrices

Any unitary can be dropped straight into a circuit — either as a raw matrix or, for a single qubit, as three tunable angles.

I.3.1

Unitaries in PennyLane

qp.QubitUnitary applies an arbitrary matrix to chosen wires, so you never do the matrix arithmetic by hand again (compare I.1.4). You supply the unitary; the device applies it.

solution
U = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
qp.QubitUnitary(U, wires=0)
I.3.2

Parametrized unitaries — the Rot gate

Every single-qubit unitary can be written with three real parameters. That general form is the Rot gate:

\[ U(\phi,\theta,\omega)=\begin{pmatrix} e^{-i(\phi+\omega)/2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} & -e^{i(\phi-\omega)/2}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\\[4pt] e^{-i(\phi-\omega)/2}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} & e^{i(\phi+\omega)/2}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\end{pmatrix} \]
solution
qp.Rot(phi, theta, omega, wires=0)
I.4

X and H

Two workhorses. Pauli X flips a bit; Hadamard makes a superposition. Combining them already reveals a gate identity.

I.4.1

Flipping bits

Qubits start in \(|0\rangle\). The Pauli X gate flips \(|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle\), so it is the standard way to initialize a qubit in \(|1\rangle\) before doing anything else.

solution
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.QubitUnitary(U, wires=0)
I.4.2

Uniform superposition

The matrix used throughout is the Hadamard. It sends \(|0\rangle\) to the uniform superposition \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) — which is why it opens nearly every quantum algorithm.

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.4.3

Combining X and H

Applying H to each basis state gives \(H|0\rangle=|+\rangle\) and \(H|1\rangle=|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Both have measurement probabilities \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\); they differ only by a relative phase the computational basis cannot see.

solution
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.4.4

A QNode with X and H — a hidden Z

The identity HXH = Z: sandwiching X between two Hadamards is a Pauli Z in disguise. It leaves \(|0\rangle\) alone and sends \(|1\rangle\to-|1\rangle\). Gate identities like this are what make compilation possible.

circuit
|0> --[H]--[X]--[H]--[M]        (= Pauli Z)
solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.5

It's just a phase

The Z family does nothing to computational-basis probabilities and everything to relative phase. Meet Z, its continuous version RZ, and the fixed fractions S and T.

I.5.1

The Pauli Z gate

Z acts as \(|0\rangle\to|0\rangle,\ |1\rangle\to-|1\rangle\). On \(|+\rangle\) it produces \(|-\rangle\). Computational-basis probabilities stay \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) — Z only flips the relative phase.

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)   # make |+⟩
qp.PauliZ(wires=0)     # → |−⟩
I.5.2

The Z rotation

RZ(ω) distributes a phase symmetrically: it sends \(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) to \(e^{-i\omega/2}\alpha|0\rangle+e^{i\omega/2}\beta|1\rangle\), i.e. a relative phase \(e^{i\omega}\) on \(|1\rangle\). Choosing \(\omega=\pi\) reproduces Pauli Z up to the undetectable global phase \(-i\).

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.RZ(np.pi, wires=0)  # = Z up to global phase −i
I.5.3

The S and T gates & adjoints

The quarter-turn \(RZ(\pi/2)\) is the S gate; the eighth-turn \(RZ(\pi/4)\) is the T gate. The adjoint (inverse) is taken with qp.adjoint, wrapping the operation before its arguments, since \(RZ^\dagger(\omega)=RZ(-\omega)\).

circuit
|0> --[H]--[S]--[T†]--[RZ(0.3)]--[S†]--[M]
solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.S(wires=0)
qp.adjoint(qp.T)(wires=0)
qp.RZ(0.3, wires=0)
qp.adjoint(qp.S)(wires=0)
I.6

From a different angle

RZ rotates about the z-axis; RX and RY rotate about the others. Watch the amplitudes oscillate and the half-angle convention appear.

I.6.1

Applying RX

RX(π) reproduces Pauli X up to the global phase \(-i\): \(RX(\pi)=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&-i\\-i&0\end{psmallmatrix}=-iX\). The same physical flip, a different global phase.

solution
if state == 1:
    qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.RX(np.pi, wires=0)
I.6.2

Plotting RX

The matrix \(RX(\theta)=\begin{psmallmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2 & -i\sin\frac\theta2\\ -i\sin\frac\theta2 & \cos\frac\theta2\end{psmallmatrix}\) acts on \(|0\rangle\) to give \(\cos\frac\theta2|0\rangle-i\sin\frac\theta2|1\rangle\): the \(|0\rangle\) amplitude is real, the \(|1\rangle\) amplitude is imaginary, and both oscillate with period \(4\pi\) — the fingerprint of the half-angle.

solution
qp.RX(theta, wires=0)
return qp.state()
I.6.3

Plotting RY

RY keeps everything real: \(RY(\theta)=\begin{psmallmatrix}\cos\frac\theta2 & -\sin\frac\theta2\\ \sin\frac\theta2 & \cos\frac\theta2\end{psmallmatrix}\). Both amplitudes of \(RY(\theta)|0\rangle\) are real, which is exactly what the matrix predicts — a useful gate when you want real coefficients.

solution
qp.RY(theta, wires=0)
return qp.state()
I.7

Universal gate sets

A handful of gates is enough to build everything. Two continuous rotations, or the discrete pair {H, T}, can reach any single-qubit unitary — the foundation of quantum compilation.

I.7.1

Universality of rotations

Rot(φ,θ,ω) is RZ·RY·RZ under the hood (and any single-qubit unitary equally decomposes as RZ·RX·RZ), so {RZ, RY} — or {RZ, RX} — is a universal gate set for one qubit. The Hadamard, a rotation by π about the \((x+z)/\sqrt2\) axis, is three quarter-turns:

\[ H=e^{i\pi/2}\,RZ(\tfrac\pi2)\,RX(\tfrac\pi2)\,RZ(\tfrac\pi2) \]
solution
phi, theta, omega = np.pi/2, np.pi/2, np.pi/2
qp.RZ(phi, wires=0); qp.RX(theta, wires=0); qp.RZ(omega, wires=0)
I.7.2

Synthesizing a circuit

Rewrite the circuit \(H,\ S,\ T^\dagger,\ Y\) — applied left to right, so as an operator it is the product \(Y\,T^\dagger S\,H\) — over {RZ, RX}. Substituting each gate's decomposition and merging adjacent RZ rotations gives a five-gate circuit, but the ZXZ Euler decomposition guarantees any single-qubit unitary needs only three. Matching that operator against \(RZ(\omega)RX(\theta)RZ(\phi)\) yields \(\phi=-\tfrac\pi2,\ \theta=\tfrac\pi2,\ \omega=-\tfrac{3\pi}4\).

solution
qp.RZ(-np.pi/2, wires=0)
qp.RX(np.pi/2, wires=0)
qp.RZ(-3*np.pi/4, wires=0)
I.7.3

Universality of H and T

{H, T} is also universal: those two discrete gates approximate any single-qubit unitary to arbitrary precision. Here the target is exact in six gates. The \((1/\sqrt2)^3\) prefactor betrays three Hadamards, and a single \(e^{i\pi/4}\) per entry says the T gates never stack. This is circuit synthesis, part of quantum compilation.

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0); qp.T(wires=0); qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.T(wires=0); qp.T(wires=0); qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
I.8

Prepare yourself

The inverse problem: given a target state, find the shortest circuit that builds it — by hand for one qubit, and by template when it gets hard.

I.8.1

State preparation

Target \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}|0\rangle+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}e^{i5\pi/4}|1\rangle\). Two gates: Hadamard for the equal superposition, then RZ(5π/4) to place the relative phase on \(|1\rangle\) (correct up to a global phase).

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
qp.RZ(5*np.pi/4, wires=0)
I.8.2

State preparation, revisited

Target \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2|0\rangle-\tfrac{i}2|1\rangle\). A single RX does it: matching \(\cos\frac\theta2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\) and \(\sin\frac\theta2=\tfrac12\) gives \(\theta=\pi/3\). Recognizing the RX signature (real |0⟩, imaginary |1⟩) saves gates.

solution
qp.RX(np.pi/3, wires=0)
I.8.3

State prep with Möttönen's method

Templates are subroutines usable like a gate. qp.StatePrep prepares any normalized vector — invaluable for multi-qubit states where hand-derivation is hopeless. Drawing at level="device" shows the underlying Möttönen RY/RZ sequence (plus a GlobalPhase) it decomposes into.

solution
qp.StatePrep(state, wires=0)
# inspect the decomposition:
qp.draw(prepare_state, level="device")(v)
I.9

Measurements

Return probabilities directly, and learn the one trick for measuring in any basis: rotate that basis onto the computational one first.

I.9.1

Measuring a superposition

End a QNode with qp.probs(wires=...) to get outcome probabilities directly. Applying H to either \(|0\rangle\) or \(|1\rangle\) yields \([0.5, 0.5]\) — the minus sign in \(|-\rangle\) is invisible to a computational-basis measurement.

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
return qp.probs(wires=0)
I.9.2

Y-basis rotation

The eigenvectors of Pauli Y are \(|y_\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). The rotation that builds them from the computational basis is S·H: Hadamard sends \(|0\rangle,|1\rangle\) to \(|+\rangle,|-\rangle\), then S puts the factor of \(i\) on the \(|1\rangle\) component.

solution
def y_basis_rotation():
    qp.Hadamard(wires=0)
    qp.S(wires=0)
I.9.3

Measurement in the Y basis

The universal recipe: to measure in a different basis, apply the adjoint of the rotation that creates that basis, then measure normally. Since y_basis_rotation maps \(|0\rangle,|1\rangle\to|y_+\rangle,|y_-\rangle\), its adjoint rotates \(|y_\pm\rangle\) back so qp.probs reads the Y-basis outcome.

solution
prepare_psi()
qp.adjoint(y_basis_rotation)()
return qp.probs(wires=0)
I.10

What did you expect?

Expectation values are how real quantum programs report answers. On a simulator they are exact; on hardware they are estimated from shots — with a spread that shrinks predictably.

I.10.1

Measurement of PauliY

Return qp.expval with a named observable instead of probabilities. Any Pauli-based expectation value can only land in \([-1,1]\), between the observable's eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\).

circuit
|0> --[RX(π/4)]--[H]--[Z]--<Y>
solution
return qp.expval(qp.PauliY(0))   # PauliY(0) is shorthand for wires=0
I.10.2

Setting up the number of shots

Hardware cannot compute expectation values analytically — it samples. The shot count is set on the device. As shots grow, the estimate converges to the analytic value \(-1/\sqrt2\approx-0.7071\).

solution
dev = qp.device("default.qubit", wires=1, shots=shots)
# shots = 100, 1000, ... 1e6  →  estimate → −0.7071
I.10.3

Evaluating the samples

Return qp.sample for raw outcomes. Since the only results are \(\pm1\), the expectation value is just their mean: \(\langle Y\rangle=\big(\#(+1)-\#(-1)\big)/N\).

solution
n_plus  = np.count_nonzero(samples == 1)
n_minus = np.count_nonzero(samples == -1)
return (n_plus - n_minus) / len(samples)
I.10.4

The variance of sample measurements

Each run gives a slightly different estimate. For \(H\) then \(\langle Z\rangle\), a single shot is \(\pm1\) with equal probability — mean 0, variance 1. Averaging \(N\) independent shots divides that variance by \(N\):

\[ \mathrm{Var}[\langle Z\rangle]=\frac{1}{N_{\text{shots}}} \]

the \(1/N\) falloff (a \(1/\sqrt N\) shrinking error bar) that the log–log plot makes a straight line.

solution
results = np.array([circuit() for _ in range(n_trials)])
return np.var(results)          # experiment
# theory:  estimated_variance = 1 / n_shots
I.11

Multi-qubit systems

Two qubits, four dimensions. Prepare basis states, measure several observables at once, combine them with the tensor product — and see why a product state's joint expectation always factorizes.

I.11.1

Preparing basis states

Qubit ordering: PennyLane indexes wires left-to-right, and the leftmost qubit sits at the top of the drawing. qp.BasisState takes a bit list, so writing an integer in binary (padded to the wire count) gives \(|n\rangle\): \(3\to[0,1,1]\to|011\rangle\).

solution
bits = [int(b) for b in np.binary_repr(basis_id, width=num_wires)]
qp.BasisState(bits, wires=range(num_wires))
I.11.2

Separable operations

Build the product state \(|+\rangle\otimes|1\rangle\) and return two expectation values as a tuple (allowed when they don't share wires). \(|+\rangle\) lies on the Bloch x-axis so \(\langle Y\rangle=0\); \(|1\rangle\) is the \(-1\) eigenstate of Z so \(\langle Z\rangle=-1\).

solution
qp.Hadamard(wires=0)          # |+⟩
qp.PauliX(wires=1)            # |1⟩
return qp.expval(qp.PauliY(0)), qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(1))
I.11.3

A two-qubit observable

Combine observables with @ for the tensor product. For the state \(|1\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\), the expectation of \(Z\otimes X\) factorizes: \(\langle Z\rangle=-1\) on \(|1\rangle\) and \(\langle X\rangle=-1\) on \(|-\rangle\), so \(\langle Z\otimes X\rangle=1\).

solution
qp.PauliX(wires=0)
qp.PauliX(wires=1); qp.Hadamard(wires=1)   # |1⟩|−⟩
return qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(0) @ qp.PauliX(1))
I.11.4

Double trouble — factorization

Measure \(Z\) on each qubit separately, then measure \(Z\otimes Z\). Because the qubits are never entangled, the joint state is a product state and the ZZ expectation factorizes:

\[ \langle Z\otimes Z\rangle=\langle Z\otimes I\rangle\cdot\langle I\otimes Z\rangle \]

so the element-wise product of the two single-qubit curves lands exactly on the ZZ curve. (You never spell out the identity part — I⊗Z is just qp.PauliZ(1).)

solution
qp.RX(theta, wires=0); qp.RY(2*theta, wires=1)
# circuit_1: return qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(0)), qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(1))
# circuit_2: return qp.expval(qp.PauliZ(0) @ qp.PauliZ(1))

II.1

The formal machinery

Part I used vectors and matrices directly. Part II needs the language physicists actually speak — Dirac notation, operators built from outer products, the groups gates belong to, and the equation that governs how any quantum system changes in time.

Dirac notation, completely

A ket \(|a\rangle\) is a column vector of amplitudes. A bra \(\langle b|\) is its conjugate transpose — a row vector, obtained by transposing and flipping the sign of every imaginary part (the dagger, \(\dagger\)). Put a bra against a ket and you get the inner product \(\langle b|a\rangle\), a single (generally complex) number measuring overlap. Put a ket against a bra the other way and you get an outer product \(|a\rangle\langle b|\), a matrix — an operator.

The completeness relation. For any orthonormal basis, \(\sum_i |i\rangle\langle i| = I\). Because this sum is just the identity, you can drop it into any expression to "insert a basis." That trick is how you read a matrix element straight off an operator: \(U_{ij} = \langle i|U|j\rangle\), and \(U = \sum_{i,j} U_{ij}\,|i\rangle\langle j|\).

Observables are Hermitian; gates are unitary

Two kinds of operators matter. An observable is Hermitian (\(A=A^\dagger\)): real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors — the possible readings of a meter. A gate is unitary (\(U^\dagger U = I\), so \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\)): it preserves length, hence total probability, and is always reversible. Every gate you apply can be undone by its dagger; measurement is the one step that cannot.

The groups: U(N) and SU(N)

The unitaries of a given size form a group — closed under multiplication, each with an inverse. \(U(N)\) is all \(N\times N\) unitaries; \(\mathrm{SU}(N)\) adds the condition \(\det U = 1\), removing an overall phase. A single-qubit gate is an element of \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\), which is exactly why three real angles (the Rot gate of I.3) describe it completely.

Generators — every gate is an exponentiated observable

Any unitary can be written \(U = e^{i\theta G}\) with \(G\) Hermitian; \(G\) is called the generator. For a single qubit the generators are the Pauli matrices, so a rotation is \(RX(\theta)=e^{-i(\theta/2)\sigma_x}=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\sigma_x\) (\(\sigma\), the Greek letter sigma, denotes a Pauli). More generally a rotation by \(\theta\) about any axis \(\hat n\) is \(U(\theta)=e^{i(\theta/2)\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma}=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,I + i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)\). A function of a matrix is defined by its Taylor series, \(f(A)=\sum_n \tfrac1{n!}A^n\), and acts on an eigenstate by acting on its eigenvalue: \(f(A)|a\rangle = f(a)|a\rangle\).

How states change in time

The continuous law behind every unitary is the Schrödinger equation, \(i\hbar\,\tfrac{d}{dt}|\psi\rangle = \hat H|\psi\rangle\), where \(\hat H\) is the Hamiltonian (the energy operator) and \(\hbar\) (h-bar) is Planck's constant over \(2\pi\). For a constant \(\hat H\) this integrates to the time-evolution operator \(U(t)=e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}\) — a gate is just evolution for a fixed time under some Hamiltonian. In the Schrödinger picture the state moves and operators sit still; in the equivalent Heisenberg picture operators move by \(\tfrac{dO}{dt}=\tfrac1{i\hbar}[O,\hat H]\) and states sit still. The bracket \([A,B]=AB-BA\) is the commutator.

The adiabatic theorem

If a Hamiltonian changes slowly enough, a system that starts in one of its energy eigenstates stays in the corresponding eigenstate throughout, picking up only a phase. This is the adiabatic theorem (Born & Fock, 1928), and it is the working principle of adiabatic quantum computing and quantum annealers: encode your answer as the ground state of a final Hamiltonian, start in an easy ground state, and drift there slowly.

II.2

Measurement, bases & interference

Measurement is where quantum weirdness becomes numbers. The rule is geometric, it depends on the basis you choose, and its deepest feature — interference — is the reason quantum computing works at all.

The Born rule, geometrically

Write a qubit on the Bloch sphere as \(|\psi\rangle=\cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle\). Measuring in the computational basis gives \(P(0)=|\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2=\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\) and \(P(1)=\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\). Points in the northern hemisphere are more likely to collapse to \(|0\rangle\), the equator is a perfect 50/50, and a measurement projects the state onto whichever basis vector it returned.

Measuring in a different basis

The computational basis is only one choice. The Hadamard (X) basis is \(|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\); the Y basis is \(|y_\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). To measure in one of them, re-express \(|\psi\rangle\) in that basis and apply the Born rule — or, in a circuit, rotate that basis back onto the computational one and measure normally (exactly what Codercise I.9 does). A neat geometric fact: antipodal points on the Bloch sphere are orthogonal states, which is why each basis is a pair of opposite poles.

Global vs relative phase — again, because it matters

An overall factor \(e^{i\gamma}\) (global phase, \(\gamma\) = gamma) cancels in every probability and is physically meaningless. A phase between components — the \(e^{i\varphi}\) above — is invisible in the computational basis but changes the probabilities the instant you measure in the X basis: \(P(+)=\tfrac12(1+\sin\theta\cos\varphi)\). Relative phase is real information hiding in a basis you haven't looked in yet.

Interference and the two-slit logic

Suppose a particle can reach an outcome by two intermediate routes. If you measure which route it took, the probabilities simply add: \(\sum_b |\langle c|b\rangle|^2|\langle b|a\rangle|^2\). If you do not, the amplitudes add first and only then get squared — and the cross terms can cancel or reinforce. That difference is interference, the same effect as light through two slits. A quantum algorithm is a carefully built interference pattern: it arranges the amplitudes of wrong answers to cancel and the right answer's to survive.

II.3

Physical qubits & hardware

The math is substrate-independent; the machine is not. Any two-level quantum system can be a qubit, and every abstract gate becomes a physical pulse on real, noisy, imperfectly connected hardware.

A qubit is a spin — and many other things

The cleanest physical qubit is a spin-½ particle: \(|0\rangle=|\!\uparrow\rangle\) (spin up), \(|1\rangle=|\!\downarrow\rangle\) (spin down). But the two levels can be almost anything with two distinguishable quantum states:

Superconducting (transmon)Two lowest levels of a superconducting LC circuit with a Josephson junction. The junction makes the energy levels unevenly spaced (anharmonic), so |0⟩ and |1⟩ can be addressed without exciting |2⟩. Today's most common platform (IBM, Google).
Trapped ionsTwo electronic levels of an ion held in an electromagnetic trap. Slow but very accurate, with all-to-all connectivity (IonQ).
PhotonsPolarization of a single photon — horizontal vs vertical. Naturally low-noise; hard to make interact.
Neutral atoms / NMR / quantum dots / defectsCold atoms, nuclear spins, trapped electrons, or crystal defects (like NV centers in diamond). A qudit generalizes to d levels.

Why anharmonicity matters: a plain quantum harmonic oscillator has evenly spaced levels, so a pulse that drives \(|0\rangle\to|1\rangle\) would also drive \(|1\rangle\to|2\rangle\) and leak out of the qubit. The transmon's uneven spacing gives a clean, isolated two-level subspace.

Topology, transpilation, and noise

Real chips only let certain qubit pairs interact — a coupling map (IBM's heavy-hex lattice; IonQ's all-to-all). Your abstract circuit must be rewritten to fit the native gate set and connectivity, inserting SWAPs and decomposing complex gates: this is transpilation, and it usually makes the circuit longer. Every gate and readout has a fidelity below 1, and each qubit slowly loses its quantum state to the environment — decoherence, quantified by times like \(T_1\). This is why today's machines are called NISQ: Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum.

Scale check. Simulating n qubits classically needs \(2^n\) complex amplitudes — about 16 bytes each. So ~13 qubits ≈ 128 KB, ~36 qubits ≈ 1 TB, ~50 qubits ≈ 18 PB, and ~56 qubits ≈ an exabyte — beyond any classical machine, which is why simulation hits a wall around 50 qubits. That exponential wall is the whole motivation for building the hardware. IBM's roadmap ran Falcon (27) → Eagle (127) → Osprey (433) → Condor (1121); Google's error-corrected Willow chip (105 qubits) is a recent milestone.
II.4

Multi-qubit gates & entanglement

One qubit is a warm-up. Real power appears when qubits combine — through the tensor product — and when two-qubit gates create correlations that no classical system can reproduce.

Combining qubits: the tensor product

Two systems join by the tensor product \(\otimes\). Dimensions multiply: two qubits live in \(\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2=\mathbb{C}^4\), with basis \(|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\). In components this is the Kronecker product: \(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\) stacks copies of the second vector scaled by each entry of the first. Operators follow the rule \((A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=AC\otimes BD\) — each factor minds its own qubit. (Contrast the direct sum \(\oplus\), which stacks spaces and adds dimensions.)

The Pauli algebra

The Pauli matrices multiply by one compact rule: \(\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\,\varepsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k\), where \(\delta_{ij}\) (Kronecker delta) is 1 when the indices match and \(\varepsilon_{ijk}\) (Levi-Civita symbol, the Greek letter epsilon) encodes the cyclic \(x\to y\to z\) order. Two Paulis on the same qubit generally don't commute; Paulis on different qubits always do.

Separable vs entangled

A two-qubit state is separable if it factorizes, \(|\psi\rangle=|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle\) — then measurements on the two qubits are independent (this is the whole point of Codercise I.11). If it does not factorize it is entangled. Running a Hadamard then a CNOT builds the Bell state \((|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2\): measure one qubit and the other is instantly determined. That perfect correlation in a single basis is classically explainable, but the correlations across different measurement bases exceed any local classical model — the content of Bell's theorem. The three-qubit version is the GHZ state \((|000\rangle+|111\rangle)/\sqrt2\).

II.5

Teleportation & entanglement protocols

Entanglement is not just a curiosity — it is a resource. Three results show what it buys you, and one deep limit shows what it forbids.

The no-cloning theorem

You cannot copy an unknown quantum state. Suppose a machine \(U\) copied any state: \(U(|a\rangle|0\rangle)=|a\rangle|a\rangle\). Feed it a superposition \(|c\rangle=(|a\rangle+|b\rangle)/\sqrt2\). Linearity forces the output to be \((|a\rangle|a\rangle+|b\rangle|b\rangle)/\sqrt2\), but genuine copying would demand \(|c\rangle|c\rangle\), which expands to four terms. The two disagree — contradiction. You can copy a known state, and you can spread one unknown qubit into an entangled \(a|0\cdots0\rangle+b|1\cdots1\rangle\), but you cannot make independent duplicates. This underpins quantum cryptography and forces the clever machinery of quantum error correction.

Superdense coding — two bits down one qubit

Alice and Bob pre-share a Bell pair. Alice wants to send two classical bits \((a,b)\). She applies one of four local gates to her half — \(I, X, Z,\) or \(Y\) — then sends her single qubit to Bob. Bob runs a CNOT and a Hadamard, measures both qubits, and reads off \((a,b)\) with certainty. One transmitted qubit carried two classical bits — because the shared entanglement did half the work in advance.

Quantum teleportation — moving a state without moving a qubit

The reverse trick. Alice holds an unknown \(|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\) and shares a Bell pair with Bob. She entangles \(|\psi\rangle\) with her half (CNOT then Hadamard), measures her two qubits, and sends Bob the two classical outcomes. Depending on what she got, Bob applies \(I\), \(X\), \(Z\), or \(ZX\) to his half — and it becomes \(|\psi\rangle\) exactly.

No contradiction with no-cloning. Alice's measurement destroys her copy, so at the end the state exists in exactly one place. And nothing travels faster than light: Bob's qubit is useless until the two classical bits arrive by ordinary channels.
II.6

Reversible computation & oracles

Quantum gates are unitary, so quantum computation is reversible — which means an ordinary function has to be repackaged before a quantum computer can call it. That repackaging, plus one phase trick, is the engine of the first algorithms.

Reversible logic

Classical AND, OR, and NAND throw away information — from the output you can't recover the inputs, so they are irreversible. Only NOT is reversible. Every quantum operation before measurement is reversible (it's unitary), so classical logic must first be rebuilt from reversible gates. The Toffoli gate (controlled-controlled-NOT) computes AND reversibly by writing the result into a fresh qubit; it is universal for classical reversible computing. The Fredkin gate (controlled-SWAP) is another universal choice.

The oracle Uf

To run a function \(f\) on a quantum computer, wrap it in a reversible oracle: \[ U_f\,\big(|x\rangle\otimes|y\rangle\big)=|x\rangle\otimes|y\oplus f(x)\rangle, \] where \(\oplus\) is addition mod 2 (XOR). Applying \(U_f\) twice returns the input, confirming it's reversible. The oracle is treated as a "black box" you may query; algorithms are judged by how many queries they need — their query complexity.

Quantum parallelism and phase kickback

Feed the oracle a superposition of all inputs (a Hadamard on each input qubit) and it evaluates \(f\) on all of them at once: quantum parallelism. That sounds like magic, but measurement returns only one \((x,f(x))\) pair, so you can't just read them all out. The real trick is phase kickback: prepare the target qubit in \(|-\rangle\), and the oracle stops flipping it and instead stamps the value onto a phase of the input: \[ U_f\big(|x\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\big)=(-1)^{f(x)}\,|x\rangle\otimes|-\rangle. \] Now \(f\)'s output lives in a relative phase — exactly the thing interference can read.

II.7

The first quantum algorithms

Put superposition, the oracle, phase kickback, and interference together and you get the earliest proofs that a quantum computer can beat a classical one — by learning a global property of a function in far fewer queries.

The Walsh–Hadamard transform

Applying a Hadamard to every qubit, \(W=H^{\otimes n}\), turns \(|0\cdots0\rangle\) into an equal superposition of all \(N=2^n\) basis states, \(W|0\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt N}\sum_x|x\rangle\). On a general state it produces the sign pattern \(W|r\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt N}\sum_s(-1)^{r\cdot s}|s\rangle\). This single operation — spread everything out, then interfere it back — sits at the heart of nearly every algorithm here.

The identity that makes it work. The interference collapses because of a bit-string orthogonality sum: \(\sum_x (-1)^{x\cdot y} = 2^n\) if \(y=0\), and \(0\) otherwise. In Deutsch–Jozsa this is exactly why a constant \(f\) funnels all amplitude into \(|0\cdots0\rangle\) while a balanced \(f\) leaves that outcome with zero amplitude. (Here \(x\cdot y\) is the bitwise dot product mod 2, and the Hamming weight of a string counts its 1-bits.)
Deutsch (1985)Is a one-bit function constant (same on both inputs) or balanced (different)? Classically you must evaluate it twice. With H, the oracle, and H again, a single query answers it — the first quantum speedup.
Deutsch–JozsaThe same question for an n-bit function promised to be constant or balanced. One oracle query gives the answer with certainty; a deterministic classical algorithm can need 2^{n−1}+1 queries — an exponential separation (a randomized classical algorithm that tolerates a little error needs only a few).
Bernstein–VaziraniA hidden bit-string s defines f(x) = x·s mod 2. Classically you recover one bit of s per query (n total); quantumly, superposition plus interference reveals all of s in a single query.

How Deutsch actually works

Start with \(|0\rangle|1\rangle\), Hadamard both. The input becomes \((|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) and the target becomes \(|-\rangle\). The oracle kicks back a phase \((-1)^{f(x)}\) onto each branch, so the input turns into \(\big((-1)^{f(0)}|0\rangle+(-1)^{f(1)}|1\rangle\big)/\sqrt2\). A final Hadamard makes those two phases interfere: if \(f(0)=f(1)\) (constant) the branches add to \(|0\rangle\); if they differ (balanced) they add to \(|1\rangle\). One measurement, one query, whole answer.

The point isn't faster arithmetic. These algorithms don't speed up computing \(f\); they cut the number of times you must ask the black box to learn a global fact about it. That query-complexity view is how quantum advantage is first proven.
II.8

Machine learning & data encoding

To learn from classical data on a quantum computer, you first have to load the data into a quantum state. How you encode it shapes everything that follows — and trainable circuits then play the role of neural networks.

The pipeline

Quantum machine learning mirrors the classical loop — dataset in, prediction out — but the middle is a quantum circuit: encode the data into a state, process it with a parametrized unitary, then measure to read a prediction. Everything hinges on the first step, the feature map that carries classical numbers into Hilbert space.

Five ways to encode data

Basis encodingA bit string x becomes the basis state |x⟩. Simple; uses n qubits for n bits.
Amplitude encodingA normalized vector of 2ⁿ numbers is stored in the amplitudes, |ψ⟩ = Σ xᵢ|i⟩. Exponentially compact.
Angle / rotation encodingEach value becomes a rotation angle, e.g. RY(xⱼ) on qubit j. One number per qubit.
Time-evolution encodingA value x sets the evolution time: U(x) = e^{−ixH}.
Hamiltonian encodingA matrix A is loaded as a Hamiltonian (block-embedded if it isn't Hermitian).

Before encoding, data is usually standardised (zero mean, unit variance) and normalised (scaled to unit length, so points land on a circle or sphere). The classic worked example is Fisher's Iris flower dataset.

Data re-uploading & variational circuits

A single qubit alone is a weak model — a gate only rotates one Bloch sphere. But if you re-upload the same data several times, interleaved with trainable rotations, the layers stack like the hidden layers of a neural network and a single qubit becomes a universal classifier. More generally, a variational quantum algorithm (VQA) runs a parametrized circuit \(U(x;\theta)\), measures a cost \(\mathbb{E}[(y-f(x;\theta))^2]\), and a classical optimizer updates the parameters \(\theta\) — a hybrid quantum/classical training loop. The VQE (variational quantum eigensolver) uses the same idea to find ground-state energies.

The swap test

A recurring subroutine: to estimate the overlap \(|\langle a|b\rangle|^2\) of two states, run a Hadamard, a controlled-SWAP, and a Hadamard on an ancilla qubit, then measure it. The ancilla reads \(0\) with probability \(P(0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12|\langle a|b\rangle|^2\), so \(|\langle a|b\rangle|^2 = 2P(0)-1\): identical states give \(P(0)=1\), orthogonal ones give \(P(0)=\tfrac12\). It is a distance measure that powers distance-based classifiers, at a cost that grows with \(\log\) of the vector dimension rather than the dimension itself.

II.9

Complexity & quantum advantage

"Quantum is faster" is too vague. The real question is how a problem's cost scales with its size — and for a specific set of problems, quantum scaling is dramatically better.

Efficient vs inefficient

An algorithm is efficient if its runtime grows polynomially with the input size \(n\), and inefficient if it grows exponentially. The gap is not subtle. Going from \(n=1000\) to \(n=1050\): an \(n^4\) algorithm slows by a factor of about \((1050/1000)^4\approx1.2\); a \(2^n\) algorithm slows by \(2^{50}\), turning one second into roughly 36 million years. "Hard" problems are the exponential ones.

But the advantage is problem-specific — quantum supremacy (or the more careful term quantum advantage) means beating classical machines on a particular task, not a blanket speedup. Grover's search over \(N\) items, for instance, needs a quadratic (not exponential) speedup, \(O(\sqrt N)\), and \(\kappa\) (kappa) in HHL is the matrix's condition number.

II.10

The road ahead

This course is the on-ramp. Everything above builds toward a sequence of landmark algorithms — here is the map of where the ideas go next.

  • Period findingSimon's algorithm The next step after Bernstein–Vazirani: find a hidden period of a function with an exponential query speedup. It is the conceptual bridge from the oracle algorithms to Shor's period-finding core.
  • Structured speedupsQuantum Fourier Transform & phase estimation The QFT finds periodicity in a state exponentially faster than the classical FFT; phase estimation reads an eigenvalue's phase. Together they are the core subroutine of the next two items.
  • CryptographyShor's factoring algorithm Uses phase estimation to factor large integers in polynomial time — breaking RSA-style encryption, and the single most famous result in the field.
  • SearchGrover's algorithm Finds a marked item among N in \(O(\sqrt N)\) steps via amplitude amplification — a broad, quadratic speedup for unstructured search.
  • OptimizationQUBO, max-cut, annealing Encode a hard optimization as finding a Hamiltonian's ground state, then reach it with the adiabatic theorem on a quantum annealer.
  • Near-term / hybridVQE, QAOA, data re-uploading Variational algorithms tailored to noisy hardware — chemistry ground states (VQE), combinatorial optimization (QAOA), and quantum machine-learning models.
  • Fault toleranceQuantum error correction Because states can't be copied (no-cloning) and decohere, information is spread across many physical qubits — bit-flip and phase-flip codes, stabilizers, and the threshold theorem: once error rates drop below a threshold, adding qubits makes a logical qubit more reliable. This is the road from NISQ to large-scale machines, and what Google's Willow chip began to demonstrate.
